## Common Lemma 1: short windows and the prime series

Each divisor gives a unit window in the integration variable, so M1=tau and
Mj<=tau^(j-1) M1. If a unit logarithmic interval contains D divisors, the union
of their windows lies in an interval of length at most two and its integral
counts all D contributions. Holder gives D<=2^(1-1/j) Mj^(1/j).

The primewise split of a divisor of ab uses exponent min(e,v_p(a)) in its first
factor and the remainder in its second. It is injective and the second factor
divides b even when a and b overlap. This proves Delta(ab)<=tau(b)Delta(a)
without a coprimality assumption.

The displayed Euler-summation identity is literally the Hall--Tenenbaum
Lemma 1 formula, checked on physical pages 5--6. Differentiating under the
convergent integral gives the negative term that is dropped. The other term
is at most sigma/[8(sigma+1)], since the logarithmic integral equals
1/(sigma+1)^2. For 0<s=sigma-1<=1 this is less than 1/(2s); dividing by
zeta(sigma)>1/s+1/2 gives -zeta'/zeta<1/s. The positive prime-power Euler
series bounds the prime-only sum. No statement for sigma>2 is needed here.

## Common Lemma 2: a uniform prime correlation

The pure terms of (sum Ai)^j integrate to a Mj. Every other monomial has
two participating indices. Pick a largest participating value and one other
factor; bounding all remaining j-1 factors by the largest proves the stated
majorization. The sum of multinomial coefficients is a^j; no hidden j-dependent
constant enters the correlation estimate.

For a fixed ordered pair of indices, a divisor contribution imposes a
logarithmic prime interval of length at most one. Chebyshev plus partial
summation bounds sum(log p)/p uniformly on such intervals, including those
touching the lower prime boundary. The weight p^(-sigma) is smaller than p^-1.
Summing the tau(m^r) divisor possibilities and integrating Ai^(j-1) establishes
the precise uniform-in-j form needed for optimization.

## Common Lemma 3: ordinary means

The inequality for f(mp^nu) is valid with or without p|m, because the divisor
bound is valid without coprimality; y^omega changes by at most max(1,y).
The identity log n=sum_{p^nu|n}log p gives the displayed convolution bound.
The higher-power prime sums are at most a fixed multiple of
sqrt(z)(log z)^(t+1)=O(z), uniformly for z>=2. The conventional divisor bound
f(n)=O(n^(1/2)) handles n<=sqrt(x), giving O(x^(3/4)); this is absorbed in
x/log x because the harmonic sum contains f(1)=1. The same argument applies
to y^omega. Thus the later ordinary-mean passage is actually proved, not
inferred from a Dirichlet-series bound alone.

## Common Theorem 4: all positive real moments

Write theta=t/j, Z=a^t y and s=sigma-1. Choosing an integer j>=max(2,t)
ensures 0<theta<=1 for every t>0. Concavity separates a Mj and W. Holder in
the prime sum yields the factor s^(-(1-theta)). Log-convexity of integral
moments between orders 1 and j gives

    tau M_(j-1) <= tau^(j/(j-1)) Mj^((j-2)/(j-1)).

The second Holder step separates L^((j-2)/(j-1)) and the squarefree series
with weight Z^omega to power 1/(j-1). Hence the s exponent is exactly

    1 - t/j + Z/(j-1) = 1 + (Z-t+t/j)/(j-1).

At j=2 the interpolation is equality and the L exponent is zero; this endpoint
needs no missing third moment. The Euler product bound by zeta^Z is valid for
all Z>0, so there is no lower bound Z>=1 hidden in this step. Its constant B
stays bounded with j for fixed r,t,y.

At the threshold y=t/(a^t-1), the positive gap is

    alpha-b = t/j - t(a^(t/j)-1)/(a^t-1).

For j>=2, j(exp(c/j)-1) decreases with j. Thus its lower bound is
[t(a^(t/2)-1)/(a^(t/2)+1)]/j. This is positive for all t>0. Increasing y
increases the gap. The source's stronger t/j bound is false: a=3,t=j=2,y=1/4
gives gap=1/2 rather than at least 1. The repaired smaller constant is entirely
sufficient for the later (Cj)^j estimate.

Set Y=L^(1/(j-1)). The inequality becomes

    -Y' <= b Y/((j-1)s) + B s^(-1-alpha/(j-1))/(j-1).

Multiplication by s^(b/(j-1)) and integration from s to 1 gives the printed
upper bound with B/(alpha-b). The integration direction is correct. L(2)
is bounded independently of j by zeta(2)^Z. Therefore
L(1+s)<=(Cj)^j s^(-Z+t-t/j), with C independent of j.

For arbitrary n, the squarefree part formed from primes of exponent exactly
one is coprime to its powerful complement. The weighted decomposition is
therefore exact in y^omega, and the remaining powerful series converges
uniformly for sigma>=1: every local nonconstant term begins at p^-2 and its
prime-power coefficients have only polynomial growth. The ordinary-mean
lemma now introduces exactly x/log x. Optimizing
(t/j) log_2 x+j log(Cj) at the displayed growing integer j gives leading
constant sqrt(2t). No early restriction to t>=1 has occurred.

If y<t/(a^t-1), T/(a^T-1) decreases continuously to zero, so a unique T>t
hits y. Weighted Holder preserves y^omega in both factors. At the threshold,
a^T y-T-1=y-1; the remaining y^omega mean has the same exponent. The resulting
exponential constant can approach t sqrt(2/T)<sqrt(2t). This verifies the
fractional range and all y>0, not just the planar application t=2.

## Common Lemmas 5 and 6: tail control and density

For 1<z<2 the expansion of z^Omega(n_Y) into divisor coefficients is positive,
and its coefficient at p^nu is (z-1)z^(nu-1). Replacing floors by X/d gives
the exact stated product, uniformly in X and Y. Mertens bounds its logarithm;
the higher powers converge since z<2. Taking z sufficiently close to 1 gives
eta=(1+epsilon)log z-(z-1)>0. The doubly exponential shells then sum a geometric
tail, uniformly in X, establishing vanishing *upper* density of tail multiples.

The finite complement events are decreasing in independent truncated prime
valuations. The covariance identity and induction over independent coordinates
prove the finite Behrend inequality. Vanishing upper-density tails make finite
periodic approximations converge both from above and below, so density exists
for S and its tails. A finite initial complement has positive periodic density
because 1 is excluded; multiplication with a tail complement of density>1/2
gives a strictly positive full complement. No unrestricted logarithmic-density
theorem is being silently used.

## Application Lemmas 1 and 2: the geometric interface

The source's planar convention really is pairwise distinct heights, not merely
noncongruent rectangles. Equal area makes widths distinct as well. The common
integer volume divides n^k, so every side is a divisor of n^k and at least n/R.
Distinct distinguished lengths bound R by tau(n^k).

Primitivity supplies a side not divisible by h. A generic parallel line through
its interior cuts successive lengths summing to n. The chosen length is <n
because h|n, and therefore all segments on that line are <n. Repetitions are
retained in higher dimensions and bounded by R. The proof only uses k as the
positive integral dimension fixed in the surrounding setup. For prime n every
side is 1 or n; a distinguished-axis line through a length-one box cannot
contain a length-n box, so it repeats the distinguished length 1, a contradiction.

For each m<=x/z, the support/coefficient lists have at most
exp(O((log x)^c log_2 x)) possibilities. Each positive sum D<=x has at most
log x/log2 prime factors. The negative exponent -log x/(4 log_2 x) dominates
the list count and every A log_2 x when c<1. Positivity of D is essential and
is supplied by the geometric primitivity step. The lemma does not count an
infinite family of prime divisors of zero.

## Application Theorem 3: planar density

For b>sqrt(x) in B2, Omega(b)<=(1.01)log_2 x and p<=z would give
b<=x^(1.01/4)<sqrt(x). Thus the largest prime exceeds z without requiring
a separate friable-integer theorem. Integers with a repeated such prime total
O(x/z). The remaining b=mp have p not dividing m.

With r=t=2,y=1/4, Z=9/4 and Z-y-t=0, so the weighted logarithmic exponent
is exactly -3/4. Removing the weight on B2 costs at most L^(1.01 log4).
The factor log_2 x in T(b)<=O(log_2 x Delta(b^2)) contributes only L^o(1).
The saving is

    2(9/10)+3/4-(101/100)log4 = 1.149842695... > 11/10.

The selected terms not divisible by p are positive divisors of m^2; their sum
D is nonzero, at most b, and divisible by p. The support count applies and
is stronger than needed. The remaining count O(x/L^(11/10)) gives a convergent
reciprocal sum by partial summation. Combined with B1 tail control, the common
density lemma proves existence of the asymptotic density and that it is <1.
This is stronger than the source's upper-density statement but does not prove
that any Mondrian integer exists or answer Conjecture 1.1.

## Application Lemmas 4, 5 and Corollary 6

The primewise sandwich (k+1)^omega<=tau(n^k)<=(k+1)^Omega and retained
Hardy--Ramanujan theorem give the divisor normal order. The logarithmic
cover of all divisors gives the lower bound for Delta(n^k). For k>=2,
log(k+1)>1, so the positive part in gamma_k(1,t) is actually
(k+1)^t-1-t. Its quotient by t tends to log(k+1)-1 as t tends to zero.
The now-reviewed fractional moment and Markov inequality prove every fixed
upper-exponent bound on a density-one set. The usual diagonal threshold
selection yields a density-one set with the stated o(1) exponent. Restricting
n>sqrt(x) changes logarithmic thresholds by fixed factors only.

Every rectangular partition has R<=tau(n^2). Its distinct heights lie between
n/R and n, a logarithmic interval of length log R. The same divisor-window
bound, uniformly for every partition of that n, gives
R<=O(Delta(n^2) log_2 n) on the retained density-one set. This proves the exact
source Theorem 2.1 without importing the unpublished power-concentration result.
