# Two homology covers in rank four

Author manuscript, phase256, 11 October 2026. This candidate proof is prepared
for independent review and is not yet admitted. Its conclusion concerns
finite groups of rank four. It does not answer the finite-cover question in
higher rank. The earlier arbitrary-cover theorem in phase216 is separate.

## 1. Statement and dependencies

A marked string group of rank four is a group generated by distinct
nonidentity involutions s_0,s_1,s_2,s_3, with s_i and s_j commuting whenever
|i−j|>1. Write G_I=⟨s_i:i∈I⟩. Throughout this manuscript G is finite and

    s_0 ∉ G123,                 s_3 ∉ G012.                 (1)

No intersection property is assumed. Put

    g=|G|, A=G012, B=G123, C=G12, q=ord(s_1s_2).

The distinctness assumptions imply q≥2 and |G02|=|G03|=|G13|=4.

**Theorem 1 (candidate).** For every prime p>4g there is a finite marked
string C-group T mapping onto G whose kernel is a p-group.

A finite polytope's monodromy group satisfies (1): the subgroup generated
by all marks except s_i preserves the rank-i face of every flag, whereas
s_i changes that face. Thus Theorem 1 would imply the following conclusion.

**Corollary 2 (candidate).** Every finite rank-four polytope whose monodromy
group is not a string C-group has infinitely many pairwise nonisomorphic
finite minimal regular polytopal covers. For every prime p>4|Mon(P)|, one
can choose such a cover whose degree over Mon(P) is a nontrivial power of p.

Problem 5 in Cunningham–Pellicer, *Open problems on k-orbit polytopes*,
§3.1, asks whether non-C monodromy can coexist with a unique minimal regular
polytopal cover. The question has no finiteness restriction. Cunningham,
*Chiral polytopes whose smallest regular cover is a polytope*, §4, expresses
the same expectation and a more specific family conjecture. Corollary 2
asserts a finite-cover strengthening for finite polytopes of rank four;
it is not a claim to settle the finite strengthening in all ranks.

The construction has two steps. First take the mod-p homology cover of a
surface with boundary; this repairs the two rank-three ends. Then fill the
middle boundary polygons and take another mod-p homology cover. The new
argument proves the precise complement conditions needed in this second
step, including the nonorientable case.

The proof reuses the cellular intersection argument and closed-surface
homology-cover theorem of phase211, §§3–5. Those arguments are restated at
the point where they are used. The raw surface and its relative collapses
were developed in the unfinished phase224_finite_rank_four.md, §§2–5; this
manuscript supplies the previously missing uniform second step. The known
existence of finite regular polytopal covers is due to Monson–Schulte. Mere
existence is not the new conclusion asserted here. The new assertion is the
existence of a cover with a p-group kernel for every sufficiently large
prime, and its consequence for finite minimal covers.

## 2. The surface with boundary and the first cover

Let W be generated by r_0,r_1,r_2,r_3, with defining relations r_i²=1 and
[r_0,r_2]=[r_0,r_3]=[r_1,r_3]=1. Let K=ker(W→G).

Construct a polygonal complex X with vertex set G and a colored edge from
x to xs_i for every i. Attach one square for each left coset of G02, G03,
and G13 along its alternating two-color cycle. The squares have embedded
boundaries. Each edge of color 0 or 3 lies in two squares; each edge of
color 1 or 2 lies in one. The link of every vertex is the path

    2 — 0 — 3 — 1.

Consequently X is a connected compact surface with boundary. Its boundary
circles are exactly the alternating 12 cycles, one for each left coset of C.
Its cell counts give

    χ(X)=g−2g+3g/4=−g/4.

The path presentation gives π_1(X,1)=K: backtracking gives the involution
relations, and square homotopies give all conjugates of the three commuting
relations. One square per coset suffices, since its boundary based at another
corner is a conjugate of that boundary or its inverse.

For I⊆{0,1,2,3}, let X_I be the connected colored subcomplex through 1,
including exactly the available squares whose colors belong to I. Its
vertex set is G_I. Set X_A=X012 and X_B=X123.

Fix a prime p and put

    V=H_1(X;F_p), Φ=[K,K]K^p, H=W/Φ.

The subgroup Φ is characteristic in K and hence normal in W. The surface
cover X_p→X with deck group V is connected and has the Cayley graph of H as
its one-skeleton. Since a compact connected surface with nonempty boundary
has H_2=0 over every field,

    dim V=1+g/4,              |H|=g p^(1+g/4).             (2)

All marks and (1) lift to H. Write A_H=H012, B_H=H123, and C_H=H12.

**Lemma 3.** The groups A_H and B_H, with their indicated marks, are string
C-groups.

**Proof.** For S=X01∪X12, every 02 square has a 2-edge outside S: otherwise
s_0∈G12. Collapse that square across such an edge; each 2-edge belongs to
only one square. Every 13 square has a 1-edge outside S: all vertices of S
belong to A, so two such edges in S would imply s_3∈A. Collapse these
squares too. Each remaining 03 square has a 0-edge outside S, for the same
reason. The 02 square formerly incident with that edge was already removed,
so it is now a free edge and the 03 square can be collapsed across it.
These collapses fix S and leave only a graph attached to S. Therefore
H_1(S;F_p)→H_1(X;F_p) is injective. The dual argument applies to
X12∪X23.

Here is the homological intersection argument used for these pairs. For
connected subcomplexes E,F with a common base point, suppose H_1(E∩F)=0
and H_1(E∪F)→H_1(X) is injective. If paths in E and F lift from the chosen
base point to the same vertex, their difference is zero in H_1(X), hence
in H_1(E∪F). The Mayer–Vietoris connecting map first shows that their common
projected endpoint belongs to the base component C_0 of E∩F. Comparing
both paths with a path in C_0 and using H_1(E∩F)=0 then shows that they
have the same lifted endpoint as that path. Thus the chosen lifted
components intersect exactly in the chosen lift of C_0.

For the two pairs above, E∩F is a disjoint union of single edges, so this
argument gives H01∩H12=H1 and H12∩H23=H2. A group generated by two distinct
involutions has the rank-two intersection property. The recursive criterion
for string C-groups now proves the lemma. ∎

## 3. Removing an end and detecting boundary classes

We first record two elementary connectivity facts with their cell details.

**Lemma 4.** The induced Cayley graph on G\A is connected. The same holds
for G\B, and for the corresponding ends in any finite marked group satisfying
(1).

**Proof.** Contract each left A-coset, retaining the edges of color 3
between cosets. The resulting finite graph is connected and vertex-transitive
under left multiplication by G. It has at least two vertices. A finite
connected vertex-transitive graph has no cut vertex: a finite connected
graph with at least two vertices has a noncut vertex (for example, a leaf
of a spanning tree), and transitivity carries this property to every vertex.
Deleting the vertex A therefore leaves a connected graph. Every remaining
A-coset is internally connected using colors 0,1,2, so a path in the
contracted graph lifts to a path avoiding A. The dual argument proves the
other assertion. Loops or repeated edges in the contracted graph do not
affect this argument. ∎

**Lemma 5.** The following complements have the same components as their
induced Cayley graphs on vertices outside the indicated subcomplexes:

(a) X\X_A and X\X_B;

(b) Y\(Y012∪Y123), where Y is obtained from X by filling every middle
boundary circle with a disk.

Here the analogous statement holds for any finite marked group satisfying
(1).

**Proof.** An outside face always has an outside vertex, and the outside
vertices of that face are connected along its boundary through outside
vertices. We check this assertion explicitly. For X_A, a 02 square is
wholly in X_A or has no vertex in A. A 03 square meets A in one 0-edge or
not at all; a 13 square meets it in one 1-edge or not at all. These are all
possibilities because s_3∉A. Thus the asserted boundary connectivity holds.
The argument for X_B is dual.

For the union of the two ends, a 02 square lying outside Y012 has no A
vertex; it meets B in one 2-edge or not at all because s_0∉B. A 13 square
is dual. A 03 square meets A in at most one 0-row and B in at most one
3-column; removing such a row and column leaves either the whole boundary,
a connected edge, or a single vertex. A middle 12 disk is wholly in the
union or has no vertex there, since C belongs to both ends.

We also need that every outside edge has an outside endpoint. For the
single end A, edges of colors 0,1,2 with an A endpoint are selected, whereas
an edge of color 3 cannot have both endpoints in A. For the top union, an
edge of color 1 or 2 with a selected endpoint is selected. An outside edge
of color 0 has no endpoint in A and cannot have both endpoints in B,
because s_0∉B. Color 3 is dual. Thus the required assertion holds.

At a vertex outside the selected subcomplex every incident edge and face
is outside it. The link of that vertex is connected, including the boundary
case in X. Hence all incident outside faces belong to the same component.
Conversely, whenever outside faces meet across an outside edge, or around
an outside vertex, their outside vertices are connected by the boundary
paths just described. Thus the components of the outside face adjacency
graph, the topological complement, and the induced outside-vertex graph
are in bijection. ∎

For a boundary circle xC, choose either orientation and denote its class
in V by u_x. To view this as the class of a based loop one may use any
path from 1 to x; the path and its inverse cancel in first homology. The
choice of orientation only changes the sign. Define

    V_A=image(H_1(X_A;F_p)→V),
    V_B=image(H_1(X_B;F_p)→V).

**Lemma 6.** For every x∈G,

    u_x∈V_A ⇔ x∈A,             u_x∈V_B ⇔ x∈B.           (3)

**Proof.** If x∈A, the boundary circle belongs to X_A and is joined to 1
inside X_A, so u_x∈V_A. Suppose x∉A. By Lemmas 4–5 the complement of X_A
is connected, including its outside face adjacency graph. The number of
middle boundary circles outside A is

    [G:C]−[A:C]=([G:A]−1)[A:C]≥2,                     (4)

because A is proper and s_0∉C implies [A:C]≥2.

If u_x∈V_A, an oriented boundary cycle b_x differs from a cycle z supported
in X_A by the boundary of a cellular two-chain c over F_p. Across every
edge outside X_A which is not a boundary edge of X, the coefficients of
the incident outside squares must agree up to the incidence sign, since
b_x−z has coefficient zero there. Choose an edge on an outside boundary
circle different from xC; its coefficient in b_x−z is zero and it is
incident with just one square. The coefficient of that square in c must
therefore be zero. Connectivity of the outside face adjacency graph
propagates zero to every outside square. But an edge of b_x also has just
one incident square, which is outside X_A, and its coefficient in b_x−z is
nonzero. This is a contradiction. The argument is valid over every field;
it makes no orientability assumption. The proof for B is dual. ∎

## 4. Avoidance of the chosen end components

Choose the lift of 1 corresponding to the identity of H. The chosen lifts
of X_A and X_B have vertex sets A_H and B_H. Above a vertex x∈A, the chosen
A-component meets the fiber in one affine coset of V_A; above x∉A it has
no vertex. Indeed, the differences of two lifted endpoints of A-paths are
exactly the homology classes of loops in X_A. The analogous assertion
holds for B and V_B. Choosing an origin in each fiber identifies the deck
action with translation by V; all these assertions are independent of those
origins.

Let E be the induced graph on H\(A_H∪B_H). We call a vertex whose image
lies in G\(A∪B) an exterior-base vertex. The distinction matters: E also
contains vertices over A∪B which do not lie in the two chosen components.

**Lemma 7.** Assume p>4g. Then E is connected.

**Proof.** A union of two proper subgroups cannot equal a group: if neither
contains the other, the product of an element in A\B with one in B\A lies
in neither. Thus there is a vertex h∈G\(A∪B).

At any exterior-base vertex x, the whole middle circle xC avoids A∪B.
All its lifts therefore lie in E. Traversing it changes the deck coordinate
by u_x, which belongs to neither V_A nor V_B by Lemma 6. Consequently one
can translate any point over x by k u_x, for every k∈F_p, using paths in E.

Consider a fixed base path γ of length L from x, and fix an initial lift.
Translate its initial point by k u_x; the entire lifted path is translated
by that same deck vector. For any visited vertex over A, the condition that
this translated vertex lies in the chosen A-component is an affine equation
k u_x∈a+V_A. Since u_x∉V_A, it has at most one solution k. A visited vertex
over B excludes at most one further value. Thus at most 2(L+1) values of k
are forbidden. Whenever p>2(L+1), one can first travel around the safe
middle circle to choose a permitted translate and then lift γ wholly in E.

Every exterior-base vertex reaches some point over h: take a base path to
h of length at most g−1 and use the preceding argument. We next show that
the entire fiber over h is connected in E. Choose a spanning tree of the
base Cayley graph rooted at h. Its fundamental graph loops have length at
most 2g−1, and their classes generate H_1(X;F_p), since attaching squares
only takes a quotient of graph homology. Let γ be any one of these loops,
with class v, and let z be any point over h. There is a permitted k because
p>4g≥2(length(γ)+1). Travel from z by k u_h around the middle circle, lift
γ, and then travel by −k u_h around the middle circle at h. All three paths
lie in E, and their net deck displacement is v. Thus every spanning-tree
loop class can be realized between points of the fiber by paths in E.
Those classes generate V, so the whole fiber is connected.

It remains to reach an exterior-base vertex from an arbitrary vertex z of
E. Let x be its image. If x∈A\B, the edge of color 3 takes x to a vertex
outside both A and B: s_3∉A, while s_3∈B and x∉B. The lifted target is
therefore automatically allowed. The case x∈B\A is dual, using color 0.
If x∈A∩B, first use color 0. The lift remains outside the chosen A-component
because it stays in its current A-component, which is unchosen. Its new
base vertex xs_0 lies in A\B because s_0∉B, so it is outside the chosen
B-component too. Apply the preceding color-3 step. This proves that every
vertex of E reaches the already connected exterior-base region. ∎

The field bound is deliberately coarse. No enumeration of H, whose order
is given in (2), is required by this argument.

## 5. Orientability and the two local complements

Let Y_H be the closed surface obtained by filling every middle boundary
circle in X_p. Equivalently, it is constructed directly from H by adding
02,03,13 and12 polygons. It satisfies all the hypotheses of the surface
construction in phase211. Lemmas 5 and 7 show that

    Y_H\((Y_H)012∪(Y_H)123)                           (5)

is connected. We must still treat its orientability and the two smaller
selected subcomplexes; connectedness alone is insufficient in odd
characteristic for a nonorientable ambient surface.

**Lemma 8.** If Y_H is nonorientable, the complement in (5) is
nonorientable.

**Proof.** For both the raw and capped surface, orientation transport along
a colored edge reverses the local orientation convention determined by the
common colored link. Hence the orientation character of a closed graph
walk is its length modulo two. In particular X is orientable exactly when
its Cayley graph is bipartite. Capping boundary circles does not change
orientability, since every middle boundary has even length.

If X is orientable, its cover X_p and its capped surface Y_H are orientable,
so only the other case needs consideration. Suppose X is nonorientable.
For a spanning tree rooted at h as in Lemma 7, at least one fundamental graph
loop γ has odd length; otherwise every graph cycle would have even length.
Its length is at most 2g−1. The construction in Lemma 7 realizes its class
v between any point z over h and z+v by a path in E. The two added middle
walks have even length, so the constructed path has odd length. Repeat the
construction p times, making a new permitted choice of k at each stage.
The total displacement is pv=0 in V. Since p is odd, this gives a closed
odd-length walk in E. Its orientation character is nontrivial, so E lies
in a nonorientable component of the complement. That complement is
connected by (5), hence is nonorientable. This argument also shows directly
that Y_H is nonorientable in this case. ∎

**Lemma 9.** Each of

    Y_H\((Y_H)01∪(Y_H)12),
    Y_H\((Y_H)12∪(Y_H)23)                            (6)

is connected. If Y_H is nonorientable, both are nonorientable.

**Proof.** We treat the first. Its selected vertex set S_v=H01∪H12 lies
in A_H. By Lemma 4, the induced graph on H\A_H is connected. Every vertex
of A_H\S_v has a color-3 neighbor outside A_H, so the induced graph on
H\S_v is connected too.

We verify that its components are the components of the surface complement.
For a 02 square, H01 meets its vertices in a 0-edge or not at all, whereas
H12 meets them in a 2-edge or not at all. Here Lemma 3 ensures s_2∉H01,
and extreme independence ensures s_0∉H12. Removing one such row and column
leaves connected outside vertices. A 03 square meets H01 in at most a
0-edge and H12 in at most one vertex. For the latter assertion, neither
s_0, s_3 nor s_0s_3 belongs to H12: the first two are excluded by (1), and
s_0s_3∈H12⊆B_H would imply s_0∈B_H. Again the remaining vertices are
nonempty and connected on the boundary. A 13 square meets each subgroup
in a 1-edge or not at all. Their selected edges cannot be opposite rows,
because all selected vertices lie in A_H and s_3∉A_H.

Finally a 12 polygon is wholly selected if it is the chosen H12 coset.
Otherwise its intersection with H01, when nonempty, is a coset of
H01∩H12=H1 by Lemma 3, hence a single boundary edge of color 1. Deleting
its two vertices leaves a connected boundary path, since the polygon has
at least four vertices. Thus every outside face has outside vertices
connected along its boundary. Every outside edge also has an outside
endpoint: a color-1 edge with a selected endpoint is selected; an outside
color-0 edge has no H01 endpoint and cannot have both endpoints in H12;
an outside color-2 edge has no H12 endpoint and cannot have both endpoints
in H01, by the end intersection property; and a color-3 edge cannot have
both endpoints in A_H. The connected-link argument of Lemma 5 therefore
completes the comparison and proves connectivity. The other complement
is dual.

The top complement (5) is contained in each complement (6). If Y_H is
nonorientable, Lemma 8 supplies an orientation-reversing loop in (5), hence
in each of the connected complements (6). They are therefore nonorientable. ∎

## 6. The second cover and the minimal descendants

For completeness, the surface criterion being used is the following. If Y
is a connected closed surface and S a connected proper subcomplex, the map
H_1(S;F_p)→H_1(Y;F_p) is injective in either of these situations:

* Y is orientable and Y\S is connected;
* p is odd, Y is nonorientable, and every component of Y\S is nonorientable.

Indeed the exact sequence of the pair reduces the assertion to surjectivity
of H_2(Y)→H_2(Y,S). Coefficients on outside faces propagate across outside
edges. In the orientable case, one coefficient is free on each component,
and the ambient fundamental class supplies their common diagonal. In odd
characteristic a nonorientable component forces its coefficient to equal
its negative and therefore to vanish. These observations give the stated
criterion, including precisely the needed coefficient qualification.

Apply it to Y_H and all three selected subcomplexes in (5)–(6). Lemmas 7–9
show that it applies. In each of the three intersections of the paired
subcomplexes, every component is a disk or a single edge: the top pair has
one filled 12 disk for each common middle coset, while the local pairs
have edges of color 1 or 2. Their first homology is zero. The homological
intersection argument from Lemma 3 therefore applies to the mod-p homology
cover of Y_H and gives all three equalities

    T012∩T123=T12,   T01∩T12=T1,   T12∩T23=T2.

The recursive string C-group criterion proves that T is a string C-group.
The path presentation of Y_H identifies its fundamental group with the
kernel of W(q_H)→H, where q_H is the order of the marked middle product in
H and W(q_H)=W/⟨⟨(r_1r_2)^{q_H}⟩⟩. The kernel of its mod-p abelianization
is characteristic, so the resulting deck cover is the Cayley complex of a
finite marked group T mapping onto H, with elementary abelian p-kernel.
Its composite T→H→G consequently has a p-group kernel. This proves
Theorem 1, subject to independent review of the complete argument.

Now suppose G is the non-C monodromy group of a finite rank-four polytope P.
The standard string C-group/polytope correspondence makes T a finite
regular polytopal cover of P. Explicitly, write the flags of P as W/J,
where J is a base-flag stabilizer, so K=core_W(J). A regular polytope with
normal flag kernel N covers P if and only if N⊆J, equivalently N⊆K.
Normality makes this condition independent of the chosen base flag.
Thus every regular quotient still covering P has its marked group mapping
onto G=W/K. Among its regular quotients still covering P,
choose one minimal under covering; finiteness of T ensures its existence.
Call its marked group Q_p. The kernel of Q_p→G is a quotient of the
p-group ker(T→G), hence is a p-group. It is nontrivial, since otherwise G
itself would be a string C-group. Thus |Q_p|/|G| is a nontrivial power of p.
Different primes give different group orders, and therefore different
regular polytopes. There are infinitely many primes above 4g, proving
Corollary 2.

The same argument also separates the universal kernels: for distinct primes
p and ℓ, the common quotient over G of the two constructed covers has kernel
whose order divides both a p-power and an ℓ-power, so that kernel is trivial.
Their kernel product in W is therefore exactly K. This last observation is
not needed for the distinct-order proof of the corollary.

## 7. Attribution, verification, and current limits

The surface intersection criterion, the careful treatment of the hidden
common deck kernel, and the passage from p-group covers to finite minimal
descendants reuse phase211 and the unfinished raw-surface calculations in
phase224_finite_rank_four.md. The newly asserted steps are the boundary
membership test (Lemma 6), the large-prime path construction (Lemma 7), and
its combination with orientability and the local complements (Lemmas 8–9).
Standard graph connectivity, covering-space theory, cellular homology,
Mayer–Vietoris, and the string C-group correspondence are used as stated.

The exact program phase256_voltage_check.py checks five explicitly supplied
marked groups of orders 32, 96, 96, 216 and 120 at primes 131, 389, 389, 877
and 487, respectively. It computes the raw homology quotient, tests (3) at
every base vertex, and checks translated paths for three specified deck
coordinates and every fundamental graph loop. Four of these groups fail
the original one-stage closed-surface criterion. The order-120 example is
A5×C2 and is nonorientable; the program also constructs a closed odd-length
exterior walk by repeating the permitted loop 487 times. These checks use
symbolic deck coordinates and do not enumerate the enormous first covers.
The four other inputs are marked-group diagnostics, with no asserted
realization as monodromy groups of actual polytopes.

The Lean file HomologyCoverAvoidance.lean proves five elementary statements:
a transverse affine line meets one affine coset at most once; fewer affine
constraints than field elements leave a permitted scalar; the specialization
to two possible constraints per visited vertex; the path-length inequality;
and preservation of odd parity under odd repetition. It uses only Lean's
standard logical axioms. The replay also rejects removal of transversality,
weakening of the strict field bound, and replacement of odd repetition by
even repetition. These formal statements verify the algebra and counting
step used in Lemmas 7–8. Cellular topology, the identification of the actual
end fibers with affine cosets, and the final polytope correspondence remain
written proofs. Neither the finite checks nor these five formal interfaces
alone proves Theorem 1.

The unrestricted finite-cover question in rank at least five remains open
within this work. This manuscript makes no assertion about new classifications
of polytopes or about how much earlier literature it replaces.
