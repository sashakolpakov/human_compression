# A common framework for marked groups, intersections and symmetry

Research note, 9 October 2026. The framework below organizes a substantial part of the Leemans–collaborator programme on regular and chiral polytopes and hypertopes. It does not encompass every conjecture in the authors' wider work. Its foundations are established mathematics; the lemmas proved here are reusable deductions whose publication priority has not been established. Internal agent reviews and exact computations are not external peer review.

The useful common problem is this: **place prescribed local groups inside a generated group, with exactly the intended overlaps, and with exactly the intended marked symmetries.** Three distinct obstructions appear: unwanted intersections, failure to generate the ambient group, and unwanted symmetry. Covers, relative positions and changes of generators act on these obstructions in different ways.

## 1. What already exists, and what this note adds

Tits' coset geometry and the C-group description of regular polytopes already supply the common language. Mixing polytopes is also established: for marked quotients of a universal group, taking the subgroup generated by corresponding generator pairs is a quotient by the intersection of their relation kernels. Cunningham develops this operation and its intersection/chirality conditions in [*Mixing Chiral Polytopes*, Section 3](https://arxiv.org/html/1111.0701#S3). Neither marked groups nor fiber products are new here.

There is an especially relevant existing generalization by Piedade and Tranchida, Leemans collaborators: their [*From Group Operations to Geometric Structures*](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.24662) extends amalgamations, HNN extensions, semidirect products and twisting to coset incidence systems. Under specified compatibility conditions these constructions preserve flag transitivity and residual connectedness; preserving thinness requires additional conditions. Their proposed graphs of coset geometries make a claim to a new universal gluing theory here particularly inappropriate.

This note contributes a concrete synthesis with several proved tools:

* an exact kernel criterion transporting an entire intersection diagram through a homomorphism;
* symmetry transport using invariant relation subgroups;
* an all-dimension matrix theorem computing the intersection of two subfield groups in every transvection position;
* an exact explanation of the coprimality condition in the PSL(2) construction;
* compatibility of rank reduction with odd-kernel marked covers;
* a complete finite search principle for rank obstructions, already used for PSL(4,3) and PSL(4,5).

The first and third tools address different mathematical mechanisms. A single quotient theorem does not turn the subfield calculation into a central extension calculation. They fit the same subgroup-diagram problem without being identical constructions.

## 2. Marked groups and faithful local data

A marked group is a group together with an ordered generating tuple. For an involutory tuple `(rho_0,...,rho_(r-1))`, put

    G_I = <rho_i : i in I>.

The intersection property is

    G_I intersect G_J = G_(I intersect J)                 (IP)

for every pair of subsets. If distant generators commute, these are the usual string C-group conditions. The correspondence with abstract regular polytopes is established; in the chiral setting one uses rotation generators and their appropriate intersection conditions instead.

One can prescribe the local groups and their inclusion maps first, form their amalgam, and ask for a completion realizing those groups faithfully. Passing from an abstract amalgam to a quotient may identify additional elements or create additional intersections. Thus there are two separate requirements: injectivity on the prescribed local groups, and preservation of their overlap diagram. Neither is automatic from the pairwise generator relations.

Coset geometries add conditions beyond the subgroup IP. In particular, a non-string C-group need not automatically produce the desired regular hypertope. For the standard parabolics of independent involutory generators, thinness together with residual connectedness does force flag transitivity: the base-chamber orbit is closed under every chamber adjacency. Thus these three conditions are not independent proof obligations in that setting. In rank three, the standard coset system is automatically a residually connected geometry; only thinness remains to be checked. The [independent proof audit](hypertope_questions_independent_audit.md) establishes these statements and their exact scope.

## 3. Exact transport of intersections

**Theorem 1 (kernel diagram criterion).** Let `pi:H -> G` be a group homomorphism with kernel K. Suppose a monotone diagram of subgroups H_I has images G_I, and that the image diagram satisfies IP. Set `K_I=K intersect H_I`. Then

    H_I intersect H_J = H_(I intersect J) (K_I intersect K_J).       (1)

Consequently the diagram upstairs satisfies IP if and only if

    K_I intersect K_J = K_(I intersect J)                           (2)

for every I,J. No finiteness, centrality or surjectivity onto the ambient G is required.

**Proof.** If h lies in both H_I and H_J, its image lies in G_(I intersect J). Choose c in H_(I intersect J) with `pi(c)=pi(h)`. Then `c^(-1)h` lies in K_I intersect K_J. This proves containment in the right side of (1); the other containment follows from monotonicity. Equation (2) is plainly sufficient. If IP holds upstairs, intersecting it with K proves (2). ∎

For three subgroups C<=A,B, the useful special case says: if `pi(A) intersect pi(B)=pi(C)` and `(A intersect B) intersect K <= C`, then `A intersect B=C`. This statement has been checked by Lean in [LiftCertificate.lean](formal/LiftCertificate.lean), theorem `intersection_of_image_and_kernel`. The formal result has exactly these hypotheses; it does not formalize every geometry or application in this note.

The quotient method itself is established. In particular, [*An exploration of locally spherical regular hypertopes*, Theorem 3.1](https://dipot.ulb.ac.be/dspace/bitstream/2013/309163/3/LSRH-revised.pdf) gives the generator-preserving C-group quotient criterion when every maximal parabolic maps injectively, extending the McMullen–Schulte criterion. The kernel formula above makes explicit what remains to check without that injectivity assumption; no priority claim is made for the elementary formula.

For finite groups, (1) also gives the exact index

    [H_I intersect H_J : H_(I intersect J)]
      = [K_I intersect K_J : K_(I intersect J)].

This is an index identity. The left expression must not be called a quotient group unless H_(I intersect J) is normal in H_I intersect H_J. When K is central, this normality follows from (1), and the corresponding quotient is isomorphic to `(K_I intersect K_J)/K_(I intersect J)`.

The theorem converts a potentially large group intersection problem into a diagram inside one kernel. If K is an elementary abelian p-group, that diagram is a finite-dimensional subspace arrangement. IP then becomes an equality of subspace intersections. This is a useful route to constructing families of covers with prescribed local behavior, but the existence of the required extension and generating lifts remains a separate problem.

## 4. Transport of forbidden and prescribed symmetries

Let W be a universal marked group and let D be a group of automorphisms of W describing allowed changes of the marking. A quotient W/N admits a particular marked change `delta in D` exactly when `delta(N)=N`.

**Theorem 2 (symmetry transport).** Suppose M and R are normal subgroups of W, R is D-invariant, and N=MR. Then

    Stab_D(M) <= Stab_D(N).

If D0=Stab_D(N) and M is also D0-invariant, equality holds.

**Proof.** An automorphism preserving M and R preserves their product N. The extra invariance gives the reverse inclusion. ∎

For polyhedra, use `W=<a,b | (ab)^2=1>` and the involution `mu:a->a^(-1), b->b^(-1)`. It is an automorphism because the defining relation is preserved. If the kernel of a cover is generated by a power of b, reversing b preserves that kernel, so a reversing automorphism upstairs would descend downstairs. This proves chirality transport without enumerating all automorphisms.

More generally, with a specified D=S3 action, a quotient whose marked stabilizer is C3 has a triality and none of the involutions in D. The theorem preserves that exact marked symmetry pattern in suitable covers. This is a conditional construction tool, not a proof that all desired triality geometries exist. Additional unmarked geometric automorphisms and the geometric axioms still require analysis.

### A reusable cyclic lifting family

Let `(G;a,b)` define a finite chiral polyhedron, with `|a|=s`, `|b|=n`. Suppose there is a homomorphism `phi:G->C_d` taking a to c and b to `c^(-1)`, where c generates C_d. Choose m with

    m divides s,             gcd(m,n)=d.

Let x generate C_m, and form

    H = <a'=(a,x), b'=(b,x^(-1))> <= G x C_m.

Then H is the fiber product over C_d, its order is `|G|m/d`, and its generator orders are `s,lcm(n,m)`. Indeed, the fiber product contains H, while `(b')^n=(1,x^(-n))` generates the entire kernel of that fiber product's projection to G. Both groups therefore have the same image and kernel.

The projection is injective on `<a'>`, so Theorem 1 gives the cyclic intersection property. The product a'b' has order two. The kernel is generated by `(b')^n`, so Theorem 2 gives chirality. Moreover,

    |H| / (s lcm(n,m)) = |G| / (sn).

Thus tightness or non-tightness is preserved. The auxiliary marked cyclic group `(C_m;x,x^(-1))` is not itself a polyhedron; this is why checking the intersection argument directly is preferable to applying a theorem requiring two polytopal inputs.

Taking `s=m=p^2`, `n=2p`, `d=p` gives the lift used in [central_lift_proof.md](central_lift_proof.md). It fills the even-exponent non-tight existence range omitted from Kong–Feng–Hou–Leemans–Qu's [accepted paper](https://amc-journal.eu/index.php/amc/article/download/3716/2232). The conclusion's printed use of “tight” is disclosed in that proof; the contextual interpretation as a missing “non-” is not silently treated as source text.

### A validation example that is already solved in the literature

The same kernel method gives a regular-polyhedron operation: if the two Schläfli entries s,t are odd, replace an involutory marking `(a,b,c)` by `((a,x),(b,1),(c,y))` in `G x C_2^2`, with x,y independent. The two new adjacent products have orders 2s,2t; their odd powers recover x,y, so the new tuple generates the full direct product. The two local kernel subgroups are the distinct lines `<x>` and `<y>`, hence their intersection is trivial and Theorem 1 preserves IP. Thus both odd entries can be doubled with a fourfold cover.

During this investigation an augmentation-module construction of type `{p,p}`, group `F_2^(p-1) semidirect D_(2p)`, was rederived, then doubled in this way. Exact [GAP checks](type2_augmentation_check.g) pass for ten primes from 3 through 31; the [log](type2_augmentation_check.log) records type `{2p,2p}` and order `2^(p+2)p`. The literature audit then found Li–Zhang's [*Regular 3-polytopes of type {n,n}*, Theorem 1.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.09505#S1), which already constructs the base family and explicitly resolves Hou–Feng–Leemans' Type (2) existence question. Therefore that question is **not an open target**, and these checks are a validation example for the framework, not a new conjecture solution.

## 5. Relative position of subfield groups: an exact theorem

Let E,F be subfields of a field L, write k=E intersect F, let n>=2, and set

    N=E_12,                  U(t)=I+tN.

Here E_12 has its only nonzero entry, equal to one, in row 1 and column 2. Let `C_SL_n(k)(N)` mean the determinant-one matrices over k commuting with N.

**Theorem 3 (transvection intersection).** For every t in L:

    if t not in E+F:
      SL_n(E) intersect U(t) SL_n(F) U(-t) = C_SL_n(k)(N);         (3)

    if t=e+f, e in E and f in F:
      SL_n(E) intersect U(t) SL_n(F) U(-t)
        = U(e) SL_n(k) U(-e).                                    (4)

The theorem holds in every characteristic and does not require finite fields or linear disjointness.

**Proof of (3).** Let M have entries in E and let `B=U(-t)MU(t)` have entries in F. Direct multiplication gives

    B_ij = M_ij + t M_i1 delta_j2 - t delta_i1 M_2j
                  - t^2 delta_i1 M_21 delta_j2.                  (5)

Entries outside row 1 and column 2 are unchanged, hence belong to k. In particular, M_21 lies in k. For j!=2, equation (5) reads `B_1j=M_1j-tM_2j`, with M_2j in k. A nonzero M_2j would give `t in E+F`; thus M_2j=0 for every j!=2. Likewise, for i!=1, `B_i2=M_i2+tM_i1` forces M_i1=0. These statements include M_21=0.

All entries except possibly M_12 now lie in k. The remaining equation is

    B_12=M_12+t(M_11-M_22).

Since M_11-M_22 is in k, the same argument forces M_11=M_22 and then M_12 in k. These zero and equality conditions are precisely `MN=NM`. Conversely, any matrix over k commuting with N is fixed by conjugation with U(t), proving (3).

For (4), U(f) lies in SL_n(F), so it can be absorbed in that group. Conjugate the whole intersection by U(-e); since U(e) lies in SL_n(E), the result is `SL_n(E) intersect SL_n(F)=SL_n(k)`. ∎

The entry argument actually proves the stronger identity for full matrix algebras, with no determinant or invertibility restriction. It therefore also gives the corresponding GL_n statement. Theorem 3 applies to a matrix group defined by equations over k when that group contains the whole one-parameter subgroup U(L): intersect the resulting SL_n identity with that group. More precisely, the outside-sum case needs only U(t) in the group over L; the inside-sum formula additionally needs U(e) and U(f) in the group over E and F, respectively. A decomposition t=e+f need not preserve extra unitary parameter constraints. The theorem does not automatically produce root subgroups of unitary or orthogonal groups; preservation of their form must first be checked.

**Projective caution.** Equations (3)–(4) pass to projective images if all scalar n-th roots of unity in L lie in k. This condition allows the scalar discrepancy between determinant-one representatives to be absorbed in the subfield group. For n=2 it always holds. Without a center check, this note makes no unrestricted PSL_n version of the theorem.

There is an actual parameter space here:

    U(E) \ U(L) / U(F)  is naturally the additive quotient L/(E+F).

The zero class gives a common full subfield group; every nonzero class gives the common centralizer in (3). This is a concrete relative-position calculation. It does not assert that all marked geometric structures are torsors, or that there is one cohomology class governing all their obstructions.

Exact GAP checks of (3) for n=3 and `(E,F,L)=(F_p,F_p,F_(p^2))` give intersection orders 8 for p=2 and 54 for p=3, equal to the centralizer orders. The [script](general_framework_check.g) and [log](general_framework_check.log) enumerate SL_3(2) and SL_3(3) completely. These examples check implementation and interpretation; the proof above is the uniform argument.

The arbitrary-dimension entrywise equivalence in (3) is also checked in [TransvectionIntersection.lean](formal/TransvectionIntersection.lean), theorem `transvection_intersection_iff`, with its [hash-bearing build record](formal/TransvectionIntersection.check.json). This certificate takes formula (5) as an explicit entry expression and proves the full matrix-algebra membership/commutation equivalence. The finite matrix multiplication identity producing (5), and the final SL_n/GL_n packaging, are elementary steps outside that certificate. The [audit](transvection_intersection_audit.md) states this boundary explicitly.

## 6. The PSL(2) conjecture and the exact gcd obstruction

Consider the construction of Leemans–Moerenhout–O'Reilly-Regueiro in [Section 6 of their 2017 paper](https://arxiv.org/html/1606.08017#S6). Allow arbitrary odd e1,e2>1 for the moment. Put

    E=F_(p^e1), F=F_(p^e2), L=F_(p^lcm(e1,e2)),
    g=gcd(e1,e2), k=F_(p^g),
    u=j1+j1^(-1), v=j2+j2^(-1),
    D^2=u^2 v^2-4(u^2+v^2),

where j1,j2 are primitive in their respective fields and D belongs to L. The precise source matrices and determinant-one normalization are recorded in [psl2_intersection_proof.md](psl2_intersection_proof.md). After a common conjugation T, their two local groups have the form

    H1=<A(u),U(1)>,
    H2=U(t)<A(v),U(1)>U(-t),

    A(w)=[[1,(2-w)/w],[-w,w-1]],
    t=D/(2uv)+1/v-1/2-1/u.                                      (6)

For each odd e>1, the pair `<A(w),U(1)>` is the full PSL_2(F_(p^e)). Here w=j+j^(-1) for primitive j. To verify this with the paper's Proposition 2.3 (Dickson's subgroup classification): U(1) fixes exactly one projective point and A(w) does not fix it, excluding a Borel subgroup. The element A(w) has projective order `(p^e-1)/2>=13`, excluding A4,S4,A5. The element U(1) has order p, excluding the torus normalizers, whose orders are prime to p. Proper subfield subgroups are excluded by w^2, a conjugacy-invariant trace square that generates the full field. Indeed if w^2 belonged to a proper subfield E0, odd extension degree would first force w into E0 and then j into E0. The PGL_2 subfield case would require even extension degree and cannot occur. These exclusions force the full group.

Suppose first that neither field contains the other. Then u^2 and v^2 do not lie in k. Set `z=D/(uv)`, so

    z^2=1-4/u^2-4/v^2.

If `xy in E+F` with x in E, y in F, apply the p^e1 Frobenius to an expression `xy=a+b`. Unless y lies in k, subtraction forces x into F and hence into k. If z=x+y were in E+F, its square would force xy into E+F, so z would belong to one of E,F. The displayed square identity would then force the other trace square into k, a contradiction. Therefore z, and hence t, lies outside E+F.

Theorem 3 now gives the exact projective intersection

    H1 intersect H2 = U(k),                  |H1 intersect H2|=p^g. (7)

The intended middle subgroup is `<U(1)>=U(F_p)`, of order p. Thus for nonnested fields the intersection property holds **exactly when g=1**. Coprimality is an intrinsic overlap obstruction, not an artifact of the original proof strategy.

If E is contained in F, then t lies in F and H2=PSL_2(F); the intersection is the full PSL_2(E). If F is contained in E, the intersection is `U(t)PSL_2(F)U(-t)`. Equal fields fall into the same conclusion. Since each degree is greater than one, these intersections are also strictly larger than `<U(1)>`.

This gives a precise theorem for the whole source family: assuming the square parameter exists, these two prescribed local groups have the required middle intersection if and only if e1,e2 are coprime. For coprime degrees it specializes to Conjecture 6.2. For overlapping degrees it proves why extending the identical construction cannot work, even if the primitive-square condition has been solved. The conditional proof of Conjecture 6.2 itself only needs containment in the two full subfield groups, so does not require Dickson's classification; classification is used here to establish the larger intersections in the obstructed cases.

## 7. Rank reduction and covers genuinely interact

Brooksbank–Leemans prove that an irreducible rank-r string C-group, r>=4, admits the replacement

    (rho_0,rho_1,rho_2,rho_3,...)
      -> (rho_1,rho_0 rho_2,rho_3,...),                           (8)

provided `rho_0 in <rho_0 rho_2,rho_3>`. Odd order of rho_2 rho_3 suffices: that product commutes with rho_0, and raising rho_0 rho_2 rho_3 to its odd order recovers rho_0. Their [Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 1.2](https://arxiv.org/abs/1812.01055) prove the resulting intersection property, not merely generation.

**Corollary 4 (rank reduction through odd-kernel covers).** Let `pi:H->G` be a surjection of finite groups with odd-order kernel K. Suppose involutions h_i generate H, satisfy the string commutations, and project to the generators rho_i of an irreducible string C-group. Assume the kernel diagram of Theorem 1 has the meet property. If rho_2 rho_3 has odd order, then both marked groups admit (8), and pi maps the reduced tuple upstairs to the reduced tuple downstairs. The same conclusion holds along any sequence of reductions whose required irreducibility and odd-order tests hold downstairs.

**Proof.** Theorem 1 makes H a string C-group. Noncommuting adjacent images imply noncommuting adjacent lifts, so it is irreducible. The order of h_2 h_3 is the order of its image multiplied by the order of `<h_2 h_3> intersect K`, hence is odd. Apply the established rank reduction theorem to both groups. Homomorphisms commute with the word substitution (8). The reduced tuples still generate the same H and G, with the same odd kernel, so the argument iterates. ∎

This links construction of covers to the rank spectrum problem rather than merely comparing their vocabulary. It does not supply the initial maximal-rank representation or guarantee odd-order reduction tests for every finite simple group. In particular, the A11 exception is a genuine obstruction: the known rank-six markings do not reduce to generating tuples of ranks five and four. The interval-rank conjecture remains open in this investigation.

## 8. Rank upper bounds as a complete extension problem

For a fixed finite simple group G, every string C-group marking is irreducible. A commuting adjacent pair would split the string into two mutually commuting nontrivial factors; IP makes their intersection trivial, contradicting simplicity.

Given a valid connected prefix `(rho_0,...,rho_(r-1))`, every possible next generator lies in

    { x in C_G(<rho_0,...,rho_(r-2)>) :
      |x|=2, [x,rho_(r-1)]!=1, x not in <rho_0,...,rho_(r-1)> }.

Retain those choices satisfying IP. This is a complete extension rule, because every prefix of a string C-group satisfies IP and distinguished generators are independent. Each extension strictly increases the generated finite subgroup, so recursion terminates without an assumed rank cutoff. Conjugacy reductions are valid only when the acting group stabilizes the prefix already fixed.

The commuting alternating generators also give an elementary bound: if the largest elementary abelian 2-subgroup of G has rank d, then any string C-group rank r satisfies `ceil(r/2)<=d`, hence r<=2d. Sharper bounds require understanding the centralizers and their possible successive extensions, not just counting involutions.

The exact implementation in [psl4_higher_search.g](psl4_higher_search.g) excludes all ranks at least five for PSL(4,3) and PSL(4,5). The [coverage proof and counts](psl4_search_results.md) explain why this excludes higher ranks as well as rank five. Uniform exclusion for all odd q remains unproved. A plausible next step is to classify projective elementary abelian triples and show that every connected extension either stays in a proper subgroup preserving additional structure or fails IP; this is a research target, not a theorem established by the two computations.

There is already a successful geometric precedent for relative-position methods: Leemans–Vandenschrick's [PSL(3,q) construction, Theorem 1.1](https://dipot.ulb.ac.be/dspace/bitstream/2013/333283/3/psl3-final.pdf), uses a local group stabilizing two lines and another stabilizing two points. Their arrangement supplies rank-four chiral polytopes for q>=5. The theorem here treats subfield groups under a transvection; it does not reprove that different construction.

## 10. Testable consequences and limits

The strongest predictions are exact and falsifiable: cyclic covers satisfying the divisibility and kernel conditions above preserve chirality and the order/type ratio; all nonzero transvection classes in L/(E+F) have exactly the centralizer intersection in (3); overlapping field degrees force the larger intersection (7); odd-kernel covers carry every available odd-order rank reduction along with them. These are proved consequences, not conjectural analogies.

They also guide failed-construction diagnosis. In dimensions greater than two, a generic single transvection leaves the whole centralizer of E_12 over k, usually much larger than one desired cyclic subgroup. Therefore a direct higher-dimensional copy of the PSL(2) construction will generally retain excess intersection. Additional relative-position constraints are needed. One must calculate their joint effect; independent twists cannot simply be assumed to intersect independently.

That joint effect is now calculated in [multiple_twist_theorem.md](multiple_twist_theorem.md): for square-zero N_i over k, parameters given by successive base-three powers of an element of sufficiently large field degree force the exact common centralizer of all N_i. Adjacent matrix units leave only `{aI+bE_(1,n):a^n=1}`; with the stated center condition, the projective intersection is the root group over k. This gives cyclic intersections over prime fields in every dimension. It does not by itself establish ambient generation or polytope relations.

The same theorem realizes arbitrary matrix-algebra centralizers after doubling the dimension: the upper and lower square-zero identity blocks force a common block diagonal `diag(P,P)`, and upper blocks containing algebra generators force P to commute with them. Applying this to left multiplication on a finite-dimensional unital algebra B realizes its unit group as an exact intersection of two conjugate general linear subfield groups. The SL version retains the explicit restriction `det(P)^2=1`. This is a further controlled-intersection construction, with the full block calculation in the linked note; it is not a claim about arbitrary finite groups or ambient generation.

For unitary groups, the involution of the coefficient field and preservation of the Hermitian form constrain possible twists. Replacing a field by its quadratic extension in (3) is not a unitary theorem. For projective groups, central scalars must be checked explicitly. For hypertopes, IP alone does not dispose of flag transitivity, thinness or residual connectedness. For simple-group rank spectra, transporting an existing marking does not prove one exists at a missing rank.

There is a concrete obstruction to the most immediate unitary transfer: substituting a primitive element of order q+1 into the known PSL(3) generator formulas does not make those three generators preserve a nonzero Hermitian form. The exact determinant certificate and its projective-scalar check are in [unitary_transfer_obstruction.md](unitary_transfer_obstruction.md). This rules out that proposed substitution, rather than resolving the PSU(3) existence problem.

General cage minimality and Mondrian rectangle dissection conjectures do not naturally reduce to these subgroup-diagram tools. Coset graphs can provide symmetric cage candidates, and short relations can control girth, but universal minimum-order bounds are an additional metric/extremal problem. Encoding arbitrary rectangles or graphs with artificial groups would add notation without explanatory power. The supported claim is a common theory for much of the symmetry-and-incidence programme, with concrete transport and obstruction theorems, rather than a universal theory of the entire bibliography.

The subsequent [36-paper compression audit](compression_audit.md) measures which published proof components this theory actually replaces and which essential inputs remain. A further [Hermitian-unital application](hermitian_unital_proof.md) uses a literal SU(2,q) torsor and elementary unipotent generation to establish the difficult residue-connectivity step in a 2005 conjecture. Its geometry-specific identification is explicit, and its priority remains under review. The general action-to-connectivity argument alone is formalized in [ResidueConnectivity.lean](formal/ResidueConnectivity.lean); the Hermitian identifications and incidence construction are not claimed as Lean-checked.
