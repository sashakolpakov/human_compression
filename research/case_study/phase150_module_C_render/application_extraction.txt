Module C: Halving and Petrial operations on regular geometries
Abstract
We study two operations on regular incidence geometries through their distinguished chamber genera-
tors and the objects retained by the operation. Replacing an endpoint involution by the conjugate of its
neighbour has an exact intersection criterion: one overlap equality and an intersection-closed family of
supports. Its geometric realization is a component of a bipartite double cover. This yields the halving
theorem for regular lattice polytopes, identifies the parity groups of generalized cubes, and gives their
spherical, hyperbolic and toroidal applications. The odd-endpoint case produces six rank-four geome-
tries for the first Janko group; an exhaustive proper-residue census proves completeness and excludes
all higher ranks. For geometric Petrials, the retained vertices and edges instead determine a possibly
nonfaithful action. Its exact kernel leads to the thin-Petrial obstruction and to faithful regularity and bid-
uality, without a finiteness assumption. The shared action and coset recognition results are used from
the companion paper Marked groups, kernels and intersections .
1. Introduction
A local change of chamber generators can alter an incidence geometry in two distinct ways. It may select
an index-two subgroup while changing the parabolic subgroups that represent its objects. It may also
identify objects that become indistinguishable after part of the incidence data is forgotten. The first
issue is controlled by subgroup intersections and flag completion. The second requires the kernel of the
induced action to be kept explicitly.
The endpoint operation replaces an involution a by aba, leaving its neighbour b and the other markers
unchanged. Section 3 gives a necessary and sufficient criterion for the new marking to have the intersec-
tion property. An odd endpoint order forces the generated group to remain unchanged; a proper halving
has index two. Both are instances of the same criterion. Section 4 realizes this change by doubling a
graph and choosing a component, with an exact parity condition on its higher shadows. This proves the
halving theorem for lattice polytopes.
For generalized cubes, the halving group is the even-weight part of a binary permutation module. Its
simple coordinate description gives all group orders, polygon parameters and locally spherical cases (§5).
An explicit Gray map then identifies the actual toroidal face posets, affine markings and translation lattices
(§6). These results recover the principal construction and applications of Montero and Ivić Weiss [MW].
The same endpoint theorem supplies the finite constructions of Hartley and Leemans [HL]. Section 7
recovers all six markings, their geometries and the exhaustive rank classification; its two complete finite
certificates use a subgroup catalogue, not an earlier list of geometries.
The Petrial operation of Bonheure, Buekenhout and Leemans [BBL] has a different local rule. Faces
are actual vertex–edge sets traced by a chamber walk, and equal sets are identified. Section 8 proves
that the action kernel is the core of the original face-changing involution subgroup. Thinness then forces
a commuting pair of generators, makes the induced action faithful and regular, and allows the original
geometry to be recovered by a second Petrial. The argument applies to infinite groups and residues. The
source’s unconditional embedding assertion must therefore be replaced by its exact kernel statement; an
explicit example shows the distinction.
Theorems U.Action, U.Geometry, U.Helly and Proposition U.ExtensionOrbits of the companion paper
[U] supply the common action and coset interfaces. All hypotheses specific to these operations are
established here. Danzer’s generalized-cube construction, classical Coxeter structure and the complete
Janko subgroup catalogue remain named inputs. The original construction results retain the attribution
above; the finite certificate interface and precise source qualifications are stated at the end.

2. Chamber actions and finite coset tests
A geometry has a finite set of types; its flags are sets of pairwise incident objects of distinct types, every
flag extends to a chamber, and its residues are the induced geometries on objects incident with a fixed flag.
It is thin when every panel has exactly two completions and residually connected when every residue of
rank at least two has connected incidence graph. Regularity means chamber transitivity of the full type-
preserving automorphism group. No finiteness of the object set is implicit.

4. Graph halving and lattice polytopes
Let Γ be a finite-rank residually connected incidence geometry, of rank 𝑛 ≥ 3 . Designate types 0 and 1
as vertices and edges. Assume their truncation is a simple graph Q, and that an edge is incident with a
higher object exactly when both its endpoints are incident with that object. For a flag F of higher objects,
let 𝑄𝐹 be its common vertex-edge shadow. All 𝑄𝐹 are connected and contain an edge.
U.Geometry supplies the connected chamber graph of every residually connected finite-rank geometry.
Restricting a chamber gallery to any set of at least two types shows that the corresponding truncation is
connected, because successive complete incidence subgraphs overlap. Apply this inside the residue at F;
chamber extension supplies an edge. The edge-shadow hypothesis says 𝑄𝐹 is the induced graph on its
common vertices.
For a connected graph Q, its bipartite double cover D(Q) has vertices (𝑣, 𝜀), 𝜀 ∈ {0, 1} , with (𝑣, 𝜀)
adjacent to (𝑤, 1 − 𝜀) when v is adjacent to w. It is connected when Q has an odd cycle, and has two
components otherwise: parity of walks proves both assertions. Choose a component C of D(Q). Define
𝐿𝐶(Γ) as follows. Its first two types are (v,0) and (v,1) in C. A higher object is a pair (𝑥, 𝐶𝑥), where
𝐶𝑥 is a component of 𝐷(𝑄𝑥) contained in C. Incidence of the first two types is adjacency, incidence
with a higher object is membership in 𝐶𝑥, and two higher objects are incident when their old objects are
incident and their chosen components meet.
The precise compatibility requirement is
if every 𝑄𝑥 (𝑥 ∈ 𝐹 ) is nonbipartite, then 𝑄𝐹 is nonbipartite, (PC)
for every nonempty higher flag F. It is automatic if the higher shadows of every flag are nested, or if Q is
bipartite.

4.2. Lattice-polytope halvings
Corollary C.Halving. Let P be a regular rank-n abstract polytope, 𝑛 ≥ 3 , whose face poset is a lattice.
Replacing one endpoint marker a by aba gives a C-group H normal of index at most two in Aut(P). Its
maximal-parabolic coset system is a regular hypertope, with full type-preserving group H. The diagram
duplicates the edge labels from b to the remaining markers, and the𝑎′–𝑏 label is m/gcd(m,2) for𝑚 = |𝑎𝑏|
finite, or infinity for infinite m. The index is two exactly when the corresponding vertex graph is bipartite;
for a final-end operation use the dual graph on facets.
Proof. In a lattice polytope an edge is the join of its two distinct vertices. Thus two vertices determine at
most one edge, and that edge belongs to a higher face exactly when both vertices do. Higher faces of a flag

8.2. The thin obstruction and all conditional conclusions
Theorem C.Petrial (Petrial obstruction and recovery). If ΠΓ is thin, then |𝑎𝑏| = 2 or |𝑎𝑐| = 2 . The
action 𝜌 is faithful and regular and is the full automorphism group of ΠΓ. Applying Π again recovers Γ
by an isomorphism fixing all retained vertices and edges.
Proof. Set 𝑃 = 𝑃 (𝐶) . Suppose first that 𝑏𝑃 ≠ 𝑃 . Both P and bP contain e and be at v, so the
vertex residue of ΠΓ contains the four-cycle e–P–be–bP–e. A finite cycle in a connected graph of degree
two is the entire graph: its vertices already have their two neighbours, so no path can leave it. Lemma
C.PetrialAction therefore says that precisely two old edges are incident with v. In the old thin connected
vertex residue both edges have degree two, and each incident face meets both. It is also a four-cycle. Its
regular dihedral group ⟨𝑎, 𝑏⟩ has four chambers, so |𝑎𝑏| = 2 .
Suppose instead that 𝑏𝑃 = 𝑃 . Since w fixes P, 𝑐𝑎 = 𝑤𝑏 fixes P and e. By the general freeness statement
of U.Geometry, the automorphism group of the thin ΠΓ acts freely on chambers. Its edge–face panel
stabilizer embeds in the permutation group on that panel’s two completions, so 𝜌(𝑐𝑎)2 = 1 . Equation
(P4) puts (𝑐𝑎)2 in ⟨𝑎⟩. The rotation/reflection intersection in ⟨𝑎, 𝑐⟩ is trivial, hence (𝑐𝑎)2 = 1 . Since
𝑐 ≠ 𝑎 , this gives |𝑎𝑐| = 2 . This proves the obstruction without presuming that b always fixes P or that

The unconditional Lemma 3.2(ii) must be read as the natural homomorphism with kernel (P4), not as
an embedding. Indeed take the complete tripartite geometry with two objects of each type. Its full type-
preserving group is 𝐶 3
2 , regularly permuting eight chambers. Let a,b,c independently change the face,
edge and vertex coordinates. The element cab changes all three, so every Petrie face consists of both
vertices and both edges. Deduplication leaves one face. Thus ΠΓ is the complete tripartite geometry
with type sizes (2,2,1), its full group is 𝐶 2
2 , and the kernel is ⟨𝑎⟩. An order-eight group cannot embed in
this order-four automorphism group. This also gives a commuting-pair diagram with nonthin Petrial, so
the principal implication has no converse in this diagram convention.
The unnumbered geometric length/order equality also cannot be unconditional. Take 𝐶2 × 𝑆3, with a
generating its central 𝐶2 and b,c distinct transpositions generating 𝑆3. The coset geometry with stabi-

connected and regular: its twelve chambers form the regular 𝐶2 × 𝑆3 action. On retained objects, cab
acts as cb, a rotation of order three, while cab itself has order six. Its geometric Petrie face has three
vertices and three edges, not length six. This assertion is unnecessary for every proof above.

theorem or an exhaustive classification. The uniform proof above covers infinite groups and residues.
