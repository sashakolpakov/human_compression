# Large cyclic subgroups in two-group polytopes

This note completes the abstract-group and presentation arguments left open in
`phase165_cyclic_index_bridge.md`. The cyclic-index shortcut and the reduction
to two marked models originate in that root-authored note; the proofs below
were checked independently and the missing center and presentation arguments
are supplied here. Historical attribution remains to Loyola's 2016
classification. No historical novelty or whole-paper compression is asserted.

All groups in the classification are finite. We write
\([u,v]=u^{-1}v^{-1}uv\), \(u^v=v^{-1}uv\), and \(D_n\) for the dihedral
group of order \(2n\). A string C-group has its specified independent
involutory marking and the intersection property. Connected means that every
adjacent product has order greater than two.

## 1. The common quotient and the cyclic-index bound

The precise existing input is **A2.3 and Corollary A2.4** of *Construction and
classification of two-group polytopes and hypertopes*, second edition. For a
connected rank-\(d\) marking, \(d\ge2\), they identify
\[
 Q=G/\gamma_3(G),\qquad |Q|=2^{2d-1},\qquad \exp Q=4.
\]
The order statement is the forest theorem on the path; its binary coordinate
law shows that every square is central of order at most two, and a noncommuting
edge gives an element of order four. This recognition is proved in A, using
U.Phi and the graded-support/descending-lift arguments. It is **not** an
unconditional recognition theorem of U.Nilpotent, which takes the canonical
quotient as an application hypothesis.

For every cyclic subgroup \(C\le G\), its image in Q has order at most four,
so
\[
 [G:C]\ge[Q:\bar C]\ge2^{2d-3}.                         \tag{1}
\]
If \(d=3\) and \([G:C]=8\), both inequalities are equalities. The preimage
of \(\bar C\) has the same index as C, so \(\gamma_3(G)\le C\). In
particular \(K=\gamma_3(G)\) is cyclic, and if \(|G|=8M\), \(|C|=M\),
then \(|K|=M/4\).

In a finite two-group, an element attains the exponent: all element orders are
powers of two and their least common multiple is their maximum. Thus (1)
applies to a cyclic subgroup of order \(\exp G\).

## 2. The rank-three reduction, including small parameters

Suppose \((G;a,b,c)\) is connected of rank three, \(|G|=8M\), and
\(\exp G=M\). Necessarily \(M\ge4\), since \(|Q|=32\). Both
\((ab)^4\) and \((bc)^4\) lie in the cyclic group K. If both adjacent
orders were at least eight, their nontrivial fourth-power subgroups would share
K's unique involution. It would belong to \(\langle a,b\rangle\cap
\langle b,c\rangle=\langle b\rangle\), but b is not a nontrivial
rotation in either dihedral group. Hence, after reversing the marking,
\(|ab|=4\).

Put \(r=bc\), \(x=(ab)^2\) and \(h=[x,c]\). Then x is an involution
centralizing a and b, and \(h\in K\). Conjugation on cyclic K has abelian
image, so every element of G' centralizes K. In particular x centralizes h.
As h is the square of the product of the involutions x,c, conjugation by x
also inverts h. Therefore \(h^2=1\).

We have
\[
 K=\langle r^4,h\rangle.                               \tag{2}
\]
Indeed its right side L is a subgroup of cyclic K and hence characteristic
there, so normal in G. Modulo L, x centralizes all generators and \(r^4=1\).
The identity \(r^a=xr\), obtained from the involution/string relations,
gives \((r^2)^a=r^2\) in this quotient. Its two basic adjacent
commutators x and \(r^2\) are therefore central. The quotient has class
at most two, proving \(K\le L\) and (2).

If \(M\ge16\), then \(|K|\ge4\), so the element h of order at most two
lies in the maximal proper subgroup of cyclic K. Equation (2) forces \(r^4\)
to generate K, and consequently \(|r|=M\). For \(M=8\), K has order two
and is central. If \(|r|=4\), then \(\gamma_4(G)=1\) and
\([G',G']\le\gamma_4(G)=1\). G' is generated by conjugates of the two
involutions x and \(r^2\), so is elementary abelian. Since G/G' is also
elementary abelian, every element of G has fourth power one, a contradiction.
Thus \(|r|=8\). For \(M=4\), K is trivial and the type is \(\{4,4\}\).

Write \(M=2^t\) and \(z=r^{M/2}\). Since h has order at most two,
\(h=z^\epsilon\) for \(\epsilon\in\{0,1\}\); at M=4 necessarily
\(\epsilon=0\). The marking satisfies
\[
\begin{gathered}
 x^2=1,\quad r^M=1,\quad [x,r]=z^\epsilon,\quad z=r^{M/2},\\
 r^a=xr,\quad x^a=x,\quad r^c=r^{-1},\quad x^c=xz^\epsilon,
 \quad a^2=c^2=[a,c]=1.                                \tag{3}
\end{gathered}
Here \([x,r]=[x,c]\) because x centralizes b; the unique involution
of normal cyclic K is central when K is nontrivial. At M=4, centrality of
z follows directly from the class-two quotient.

## 3. Exact models and their exponent

For M≥8 and either epsilon, or M=4 and epsilon=0, start with
\(D=\langle x,z\rangle\cong C_2^2\). Let r of order M act by
\(x^r=xz^\epsilon\), fixing z, and set
\[
 N=(D\rtimes C_M)/\langle z r^{-M/2}\rangle.
\]
The identified subgroup is central of order two and meets both factors
trivially. Thus \(|N|=2M\), \(|r|=M\), and its unique normal forms are
\(x^u r^v\), \(u\in\{0,1\}\), \(v\in\mathbb Z/M\).
In particular \(x\notin\langle r\rangle\).

Define A,C on N by the a,c formulas in (3). They preserve all relations and
satisfy \(A^2=C^2=1\), \(AC=CA\). The parameter-sensitive relation is
\[
 (xr)^{M/2}=r^{M/2}z^{\epsilon\binom{M/2}{2}}.
\]
The binomial coefficient is even for M≥8; at M=4 it is odd and epsilon=0
is required. Compatibility with \([x,r]=z^\epsilon\) follows by direct
substitution. Both composites send r to \(r^{-1}x\) and x to
\(xz^\epsilon\); squaring either automorphism fixes r and x.
Consequently
\[
 G_\epsilon=N\rtimes\langle a,c\rangle,
 \qquad \langle a,c\rangle\cong C_2^2,
 \qquad b=rc                                                   \tag{4}
\]
has order 8M. The markers a,b,c are involutions, commute at the distant
pair, satisfy \((ab)^2=x\), and generate (4). Their adjacent orders are
4 and M.

Their intersection property is explicit. Projection onto \(G_\epsilon/N\)
confines \(\langle a,b\rangle\cap\langle b,c\rangle\) to
\(1,x,b,xb\). Since x is not a power of r, only 1,b remain.
Also \(\langle a,c\rangle=\{1,a,c,ac\}\); the normal forms show its
intersections with the other two maximal parabolics are exactly
\(\langle a\rangle\) and \(\langle c\rangle\). Rank-two independence
and these three intersections give every marked-subset intersection.

For completeness, this model has exponent M, not merely an adjacent element
of that order. In N,
\[
 (x^u r^v)(x^{u'}r^{v'})
  =x^{u+u'}r^{v+v'}z^{\epsilon v u'},                    \tag{5}
\]
with binary x exponent. This gives exponent dividing M. For \(n=x^u r^v\),
the squares in its three other cosets satisfy
\[
 (na)^2\in x^v r^{2v}\langle z\rangle,
 \qquad (nc)^2\in\langle z\rangle,
 \qquad (nac)^2\in\langle x,z\rangle.
\]
The first expression has order dividing M/2 because M/2 is even and
x commutes with \(r^{2v}\). The other squares have order at most two.
Thus every element has order dividing M, and r attains it. Conversely, (3)
gives a surjection from this model onto every original G in §2. Equal order
8M makes it an isomorphism of marked groups. This extends the s=3 model of
Module A §5 to all required low parameters without using its n≥10 convention.

## 4. Unmarked nonisomorphism

For M≥8,
\[
 Z(G_0)=\langle x,z\rangle\cong C_2^2,
 \qquad Z(G_1)=\langle z\rangle\cong C_2.               \tag{6}
\]
To prove this, write an element uniquely as
\(g=x^u r^v a^\alpha c^\gamma\). If \(\alpha=1\), conjugation of r
by g has a nonzero x coordinate, so cannot equal r. If \(\alpha=0\)
but \(\gamma=1\), equality would require
\(r^2=z^{\epsilon u}\), impossible because \(|r^2|\ge4\). Therefore
every central element belongs to N. For epsilon=0, x is central and
commutation with c forces \(2v=0\pmod M\), giving exactly \(\langle
x,z\rangle\). For epsilon=1, commutation with r first forces u=0;
commutation with c then again forces \(v=0\) or M/2. Centrality of z was
already established. This proves (6), so the two groups are nonisomorphic
even after forgetting all marker labels. The single M=4 model has center
of order four.

## 5. The literal source presentations

Let \(P_\epsilon\) be the group given by Loyola's Theorem4.1(5):
\[
\left\langle a,b,c\ \middle|\ a^2=b^2=c^2=(ac)^2=(ab)^4=(bc)^M=1,
 (abcb)^2(cb)^{\epsilon M/2}=1\right\rangle.             \tag{7}
\]
The exact bridge is a word identity in the common Coxeter relations, before
any finite-order bound or centrality of z is assumed. With x=(ab)² and
h=[x,c],
\[
\begin{aligned}
 h^b&=babab\,c\,abab\,cb\\
    &=aba\,c\,abab\,cb\\
    &=abcbabcb=(abcb)^2.                                \tag{8}
\end{aligned}
\]
The second equality uses \((ab)^4=1\), and the third uses aca=c.
Also b inverts r=bc and fixes \(z=r^{M/2}\). Therefore the extra relation
in (7) is equivalent to \(h=z^\epsilon\), in both directions.

It follows in (7) that x²=1, \([x,r]=z^\epsilon\), and all relations
(3) hold. For epsilon=1, a fixes both x,c and therefore fixes h=z; b,c
fix z as the half-rotation. Thus z is central. For epsilon=0, x centralizes
a,b,c, and \(z^a=(xr)^{M/2}=z\), so z is central in this case too.
Moving a,c to the right and collecting x,r now gives at most the 8M
normal forms in (4). The concrete model satisfies every relator of (7)
and has 8M elements. Hence (7) defines precisely \(G_\epsilon\).
This proves the universal source presentation, not only the existence of
a quotient with the desired order or IP.

These are smooth quotients of the infinite string Coxeter group [4,M]:
the adjacent orders are exactly 4 and M. Infinitude needs no catalogue.
Since 4 divides M, [4,M] maps onto [4,4], whose affine square model in
Module A §3 contains the two independent unit translations.

For the source's original three-generator presentation, rename its third
generator \(\rho\), to distinguish it from our rotation r. The presentation
on its printed p.13 is
\[
\begin{gathered}
 p^M=q^4=\rho^2=1,\qquad
 q^{-1}pq=q^2p^{-1},\qquad q^{-2}pq^2=p^{1+\epsilon M/2},\\
 \rho^{-1}p\rho=q^2p,\qquad \rho^{-1}q\rho=q^{-1}.      \tag{9}
\end{gathered}
The forward substitution is
\[
 p=bc=r,\qquad q=ab,\qquad \rho=a.                     \tag{10}
\]
For example \(p^q=xr^{-1}\), \(p^{q^2}=rz^\epsilon\),
\(p^\rho=xr\), and \(q^\rho=q^{-1}\); thus every relator of (9)
holds in (7).

Conversely, in the group (9), put
\[
 a=\rho,\qquad b=q^{-1}\rho,\qquad
 c=p^{-1}q^{-1}\rho.                                   \tag{11}
\]
The conjugation relations give b²=c²=1, \(ac=p^{-1}q^{-1}\) and
\((ac)^2=1\), while ab=q and bc=p. For clarity, the c²/ac calculation
uses
\(q^{-1}p^{-1}q=pq^{-2}\), so
\(p^{-1}q^{-1}p^{-1}q^{-1}=1\). Set x=q² and z=p^(M/2).
Then x centralizes b, and \(p^x=pz^\epsilon\) implies
\([x,p]=z^\epsilon\), since z is the half-rotation fixed by x.
As c=bp, this gives \([x,c]=z^\epsilon\). Equation (8) supplies
the last relator of (7). Substitutions (10) and (11) are inverse on all
generators, proving an actual bidirectional presentation isomorphism.

For m≥7, M=2^(m−3), the source names are
\(\mathcal M_{II-D,19}(m)=G_0\) and
\(\mathcal M_{II-D,3}(m)=G_1\). The same marked presentations give
the two M=8 boundary groups and the single M=4 group without relying on
SmallGroups identifiers or the source's separate abstract-group catalogue.

## 6. The exact product decompositions and source correction

Put \(d=r^2\) and
\[
 L=\langle c,d\rangle\cong D_{M/2},\qquad
 H=\langle a,x\rangle\cong C_2^2=D_2.
\]
Their intersection is trivial by the normal forms. H normalizes L, with
the complete right-conjugation actions
\[
 d^a=dz^\epsilon,\quad c^a=c,\qquad
 d^x=d,\quad c^x=cz^\epsilon,
 \qquad z=d^{M/4}.                                    \tag{12}
\]
Thus LH has order 4M, is an internal semidirect product, and is normal
in G. The element b lies outside LH: elements of LH have even r-coordinate,
whereas b=rc has odd r-coordinate. Conjugation by b is
\[
 d^b=d^{-1},\quad c^b=dc,\quad a^b=xa,\quad x^b=x.    \tag{13}
\]
Consequently
\[
 G_0\cong(D_{M/2}\times D_2)\rtimes C_2,
 \qquad
 G_1\cong(D_{M/2}\rtimes D_2)\rtimes C_2,              \tag{14}
\]
where (12) and (13) specify the actions, rather than an ambiguous
semidirect-product symbol. For epsilon=1 the inner action is nontrivial
because z is nonidentity. For epsilon=0 it is trivial. These formulas
agree with source Proposition4.2 and its conclusion on printed p.17.
**The final parameter assignment in Theorem4.1(5), printed p.11, is reversed:**
its direct first factor belongs to e=0 and its nontrivial semidirect first
factor belongs to e=1. This is a stated correction, without a correction-priority
claim.

## 7. The disconnected cases and the exact recovered contract

Suppose G has any string C-group marking and \(|G|/\exp G\le8\).
Different connected components commute, and IP makes them an internal direct
product. A component of rank at least three has cyclic index at least eight.
If it attains the exponent, any other nontrivial component makes the total
ratio at least16. If it does not, choose a different component attaining the
exponent; the rank-three-or-higher factor itself has order at least32, again
making the ratio exceed eight. Thus a disconnected marking has only single
involutions and connected dihedral components.

If all components are single involutions, G=C₂^d has ratio 2^(d−1), so
only d≤4 occurs. Otherwise select a dihedral component with maximum rotation
order E=exp G≥4. Its order is2E, and every other connected nontrivial
dihedral component has order at least8, which would already make the total
ratio at least16. Hence all other components are single involutions; there
are at most two of them. Precisely
\[
 G\cong D_E\times C_2^j,\qquad j=0,1,2,
 \qquad |G|/\exp G=2^{j+1}.                            \tag{15}
\]
The elementary-abelian boundary cases agree with the same notation using
\(D_2=C_2^2\), alongside G=C₂ at ratio1. For a connected marking of
rank≥3, inequality (1) forces rank3 and ratio8; §§2–6 give exactly the
groups (7). Rank1 is C₂ and rank2 is dihedral. This proves every clause
of Loyola's Theorem4.1, with the correction in (14), and the structural
presentation/decomposition content of Proposition4.2.

This is a complete deduction of that C-group classification, not a recovery
of the whole paper. Loyola's independent correction/completion of the 123
rank-three and 14 rank-four abstract-group lists, all their involution tables
and the individual excluded-group catalogue statements are not claimed as
consequences. The new deduction does not import those catalogues. Its substantive
shared recognition input is the proved A2.3/A2.4 forest quotient; the all-parameter
model argument is supplied above. Finite computations are corroboration, not
a replacement for these uniform proofs. No whole-theorem Lean certificate,
matched compression ratio or historical novelty is claimed here.
