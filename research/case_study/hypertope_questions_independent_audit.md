# Independent audit of the proposed answers to Problems 9.3 and 9.4

9 October 2026. This is an internal adversarial proof audit, not external peer review or a claim about publication priority. Source: Fernandes–Leemans–Ivić Weiss, *Highly symmetric hypertopes*, §2–4 and Problems 9.3–9.4 in §9. The [accepted author manuscript](https://ria.ua.pt/bitstream/10773/16681/1/2016DFW.pdf) and local [extracted preprint](sources/README.md) use the standard coset construction described below.

Both proposed negative answers are correct for that construction. Neither depends on a computational search. The hypotheses about which subgroups define the geometry are essential to stating the result accurately.

## Definitions and scope

Let I be a finite type set, let `G=<rho_i:i in I>` and put `G_i=<rho_j:j!=i>`. Vertices of type i are the right cosets `G_i g`; two vertices of different types are incident if their cosets intersect. Types remain part of the vertex data even if some subgroups coincide. Let `C={G_i:i in I}` be the base chamber. This is the construction from a C-group used in the paper's §4. The group itself need not be finite.

In §2, an incidence geometry means that every flag extends to a chamber. Thinness means that each panel has exactly two chamber completions. Residual connectedness requires every residue of rank at least two, including the empty-flag residue, to have connected incidence graph. A C-group has independent generators: `rho_i` is not in `G_i`, by applying IP to `{i}` and its complement.

The statements below do not address a C-group equipped with unrelated arbitrary subgroups. In that broader interpretation the distinguished generators need not induce the required chamber adjacencies. The paper explicitly uses the standard parabolics above immediately before its questions.

## Problem 9.3: thin and residually connected forces flag transitivity

**Claim.** Suppose the standard coset incidence system is a thin, residually connected geometry and the generators are independent. Then G acts transitively on its chambers and on flags of each type. In particular, the example requested in Problem 9.3 cannot exist.

**Proof.** For every i, `C rho_i` agrees with C in all types other than i, since `rho_i` belongs to `G_j` for `j!=i`. The i-vertex changes because `rho_i` does not belong to `G_i`. Consequently `C rho_i` is an i-adjacent chamber. Thinness says that it is the unique i-adjacent chamber.

For every g in G, the unique i-adjacent chamber to `C g` is therefore `C rho_i g`, which is again in the G-orbit of C. This orbit is closed under every chamber adjacency. A residually connected geometry has connected chamber graph, so that orbit comprises all chambers. Every flag extends to a chamber, and a type-preserving map between two extending chambers maps the corresponding flags. Thus G is flag-transitive. ∎

Here is an independent proof of the connectivity step, to avoid importing a stronger assertion than is warranted. Induct on the finite rank. Rank two is immediate from incidence-graph connectivity. At higher rank, each vertex residue is residually connected and is a geometry, hence its chamber graph is connected by induction. Chambers containing a fixed vertex can therefore be joined while fixing that vertex. A path in the incidence graph joining vertices of two chosen chambers can be covered by chambers, because each incident pair extends to a chamber. Consecutive covering chambers share a vertex and can be joined as just proved. Concatenation gives a chamber path.

Thinness is needed for closure of the orbit under all adjacencies. Residual connectedness is needed for the induction. Mere incidence-graph connectivity and firmness do not imply that vertex residues are connected. The printed proof of the paper's Proposition 2.1 includes a sentence suggesting that implication, but its stated theorem is correct under its actual residual-connectedness hypothesis; the argument above supplies the required implication directly.

For a C-group, the base-chamber stabilizer is `intersection_i G_i=1`, again by IP. Thus the G-action is in fact regular on chambers. This strengthening is not required to answer the question.

## Problem 9.4: rank-three standard coset systems are residually connected

**Claim.** Let G be generated by three elements, with standard pair-generated parabolics `G_i=<rho_j,rho_k>`. Its coset incidence system is a residually connected geometry. The generators need not be involutions or independent, and no IP hypothesis is needed. Therefore it cannot furnish the non-residually-connected example requested in Problem 9.4.

**Geometry.** Any incident pair of cosets has a common element h. Adding the coset `G_k h` of the third type extends the pair to a chamber. Singletons extend similarly. Hence every flag extends, independently of any transitivity assumption.

**Whole incidence graph.** The parabolics generate G. Each translated base chamber is a clique, and chambers translated by an element of a parabolic share a vertex. Factoring any element of G as a product of elements of the parabolics joins its translated base chamber to C. Every vertex occurs in one of these chambers, so the entire incidence graph is connected. There may also be chambers outside this orbit; they are immaterial to vertex connectivity.

**Singleton residues.** By typewise G-transitivity it suffices to consider the residue of `G_i`. Write `A=G_i intersect G_j` and `B=G_i intersect G_k`. Every incident j-vertex has a representative in `G_i`: if `G_j x` meets `G_i`, choose h in the intersection and write it as `G_j h`. Thus the j-vertices identify bijectively with the right A-cosets in `G_i`. The k-vertices likewise identify with the B-cosets.

The usual bipartite coset graph on A- and B-cosets inside `G_i` is a spanning subgraph of this residue: an intersection inside `G_i` is certainly an intersection in G. It is connected because `G_i=<A,B>`. Indeed, `rho_k` belongs to A and `rho_j` belongs to B, and those two elements generate `G_i`. The elementary coset-graph criterion follows by multiplying successively by elements of A and B; its components are precisely indexed by the cosets of `<A,B>`.

Rank three has no further residues of rank at least two beyond the empty-flag and singleton residues. All are connected, establishing the claim. ∎

## Checks against plausible failure modes

* A missing chamber-extension hypothesis does not affect the rank-three result: it was proved directly. It is explicit in Problem 9.3's use of the word geometry.
* The actual singleton residue may have more edges than the internal coset graph. Extra edges cannot destroy connectivity, so equality of those graphs is unnecessary.
* No unjustified flag-transitivity assumption is used in the residue argument. Only the obvious transitivity on vertices of a fixed type is used to choose the standard residue.
* The rank-three assertion is compatible with the paper's Example 4.4: its incidence graph is `K_(3,3,3)`, which is residually connected even though the specified G is not chamber-transitive. It does not assert thinness or transitivity.
* The rank-four example in §4 can have disconnected residues of flags of size two. The rank-three argument has no such residues to control and therefore does not contradict that example.

These short results eliminate the two requested kinds of examples under the stated standard construction. They do not turn every C-group coset system into a regular hypertope: thinness and, in higher rank, the remaining residue conditions can still fail.
