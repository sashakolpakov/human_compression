Marked groups, kernels and intersections
Abstract
Many constructions of incidence geometries require the same two tasks: control the intersections of
subgroups generated by selected markers, and identify the permutation group acting on the resulting
objects. We give a common treatment in which a quotient supplies the visible geometry and its kernel
records the remaining intersection obstructions. The main construction assembles local extensions over
specified sets of markers. Injectivity on every proper local parabolic is enough to isolate the entire
local kernels, even when their supports are nested. It follows that the full fiber product is generated by
the prescribed markers and has an exact kernel profile on every parabolic. We derive local recognition,
deletion maps, lower-central identities and separator factorizations from the same proof. Exact coordinate
profiles also transfer typed coset Helly; with the necessary involutory local markings this gives regular
geometries. A finite nilpotent quotient and an invariant subgroup ladder then give a complete parameter
space for marked finite two-group quotients with a prescribed class-two quotient. Further results treat
exact quotient intersections, comparison through one exceptional parabolic, faithful actions on retained
objects, relative generation and Frattini coordinates. These results are intended to be cited with explicit
hypothesis bindings; local presentations, geometric identifications and finite classifications remain the
responsibility of their applications.
1. Markings, images and kernels
A marked group is a group 𝐺 together with a finite indexed generating family (𝑟𝑖)𝑖∈𝐼. Except where
stated, the markers need not be involutions or distinct. For 𝑆 ⊆ 𝐼 , put
𝐺𝑆 = ⟨𝑟 𝑖 ∶ 𝑖 ∈ 𝑆⟩, 𝐺 ∅ = 1.
We call these subgroups parabolics, retaining their index sets even when two of them happen to coincide.
The marking has the intersection property, or IP, if
𝐺𝑆 ∩ 𝐺𝑇 = 𝐺 𝑆∩𝑇 (𝑆, 𝑇 ⊆ 𝐼). (1.1)
A marked homomorphism sends each source marker to the corresponding target marker. Its image on
𝐺𝑆 is therefore the target parabolic with index set 𝑆.
All subgroup products below are products of sets unless a subgroup assertion is made. We write𝛾1(𝐺) =
𝐺 and 𝛾𝑗+1(𝐺) = [𝛾 𝑗(𝐺), 𝐺]. We use the elementary correspondence and isomorphism theorems for
groups. In the finite 𝑝-group arguments we retain Burnside’s basis theorem, the nilpotence of finite 𝑝-
groups, and the usual multilinear commutator description of the lower-central graded Lie ring. Precisely,
its degree-one part generates the graded Lie ring, and degree 𝑗 is generated by left-normed commutators
of weight 𝑗 in any generating set. In Section 8 we explicitly retain the normal-form theorem for an
amalgamated free product. None of these classical inputs is presented as a result originating in this
manuscript.

The next result concerns the opposite direction. Passing to a quotient can create new intersections, and
this defect is a product condition in its kernel.

7. How the statements are used
The coordinate-kernel theorem U.SK.Helly additionally requires exact kernel profiles and base IP to
move pairwise intersections to a common fiber. Its geometric corollary requires actual involutory local
markers, as well as a regular base geometry. These obligations are checked for the three dihedral pair
attachments in Module A.
The application interface is mathematical rather than merely notational. An application of U.SK supplies
the marked base, every actual deletion, each local marked epimorphism, and injectivity on every proper
local parabolic. Its order formula then needs only the orders of the base and local kernels. Its universal-
presentation claim, if any, still requires a presentation map and an independent sharp bound such as
U.Order. An application of U.Nilpotent must first identify the canonical class-two quotient of every
target group. An application of U.Compare must prove injectivity on the whole protected product set and
exact membership in the exceptional image. An application of U.Action supplies the actual encoding
and retained surjection, not an assumed equality of kernels.
These requirements permit the same complete proof to be cited several times without repeating its induc-
tion or quotient argument. They also identify the portions that cannot be omitted from an application:
local models, presentation recognition, geometric realization, source-specific parameter ranges, and any
exhaustive finite computation on which its result depends. The companion contract ledger records these
bindings separately from the historical attribution of each original theorem.
8. Genuine group pushouts and primary torsion
Fix injective homomorphisms 𝑖𝐴 ∶ 𝐶 → 𝐴 and 𝑖𝐵 ∶ 𝐶 → 𝐵 . Let 𝐴 ∗ 𝐵 be the free product and let
𝑃 = (𝐴 ∗ 𝐵)/⟨ ⟨𝑖𝐴(𝑐)𝑖𝐵(𝑐)−1 ∶ 𝑐 ∈ 𝐶⟩ ⟩. (8.1)
We use the amalgam normal-form theorem in the following precise form: the canonical maps of 𝐴
and 𝐵 into 𝑃 are injective; an alternating reduced word with factors outside the common copy of 𝐶
has nonzero reduced length and is nonidentity; every element is conjugate either into a factor or to a
cyclically reduced alternating word of length at least two, whose positive powers remain reduced with
proportionally increasing length. This is a retained classical theorem.
Theorem U.Amalgam (universal property and torsion). The group 𝑃 in (8.1), with its canonical maps
𝑗𝐴, 𝑗𝐵, is a pushout of 𝐴 ← 𝐶 → 𝐵 in the category of groups: for every group 𝐷 and homomorphisms
𝑓𝐴 ∶ 𝐴 → 𝐷 , 𝑓𝐵 ∶ 𝐵 → 𝐷 satisfying 𝑓𝐴𝑖𝐴 = 𝑓 𝐵𝑖𝐵, there is a unique homomorphism 𝑓 ∶ 𝑃 → 𝐷
with 𝑓𝑗 𝐴 = 𝑓 𝐴 and 𝑓𝑗 𝐵 = 𝑓 𝐵.
Let 𝑝 be a prime. Say that a group has 𝑝-primary finite torsion when every finite-order element has
𝑝-power order. This property passes to subgroups, to extensions with finite 𝑝-group quotient, and to
amalgamated free products of groups having the property. If 𝑖𝐴(𝐶) ≠ 𝐴 and 𝑖𝐵(𝐶) ≠ 𝐵 , then 𝑃 is
infinite. If 𝐴 and 𝐵 are finitely generated, then so is 𝑃 .
Proof. The free-product universal property gives a unique homomorphism𝐴∗𝐵 → 𝐷 extending 𝑓𝐴, 𝑓𝐵.
Agreement on 𝐶 puts every defining relator in its kernel, so it factors through 𝑃 . The images of 𝐴 and
𝐵 generate 𝑃 ; hence this factorization is unique. The equality 𝑗𝐴𝑖𝐴 = 𝑗 𝐵𝑖𝐵 follows from the defining
relations, completing the pushout property.
The torsion assertion for subgroups is immediate. For an extension 1 → 𝐾 → 𝐸 → 𝑅 → 1 , with
𝑅 a finite 𝑝-group and 𝐾 having 𝑝-primary finite torsion, take any finite cyclic subgroup ⟨𝑥⟩ of 𝐸. Its
intersection with 𝐾 is a cyclic 𝑝-group, and its image in 𝑅 is a 𝑝-group. The order formula for this
restricted exact sequence makes |𝑥| a power of 𝑝.
For an amalgam, the normal-form theorem places a torsion element, up to conjugacy, in a factor: the
alternative cyclically reduced word has powers of unbounded reduced length and cannot have finite order.
The factor hypothesis therefore proves the torsion assertion. If the common subgroup is proper in both
factors, choose 𝑎 ∈ 𝐴 ∖ 𝑖 𝐴(𝐶) and 𝑏 ∈ 𝐵 ∖ 𝑖 𝐵(𝐶). The words (𝑎𝑏)𝑛 are reduced of length 2𝑛, so are
all distinct; this proves infinitude. Finally, the images of finite generating sets of 𝐴 and 𝐵 generate their
free product and its quotient 𝑃 , proving finite generation. □
The simultaneous fiber product of U.SK has its displayed compatibility equations and its proved gener-
ation theorem. It is not the pushout in U.Amalgam. A graph of proof dependencies is neither of these
group constructions.

Module A: Construction and classification of two-group polytopes and hy-
pertopes
Abstract
We develop construction and classification methods for regular polytopes and hypertopes whose auto-
morphism groups have two-power order. A forest of noncommuting generators determines a canonical
class-two quotient. Its square-lattice sections provide local kernels that can be attached independently
by the supported-kernel theorem of Marked groups, kernels and intersections . This yields prescribed
edge orders, square sections and three-generator hypertope types, with exact orders and complete pre-
sentations. Conversely, a fixed class-two quotient reduces bounded-order classification to an exhaustive
invariant-kernel problem. We combine this reduction with cyclic-core and central-descent arguments to
recover the high-type rank-three families and the full order-1024 classification, including named facets
and corrected presentations. The same local models also support regular, chiral and alternating exten-
sions. Source conclusions, retained geometric inputs and corrections are stated explicitly.
Introduction
Local sections serve two complementary purposes in finite incidence geometry. One may prescribe them
and construct a marked group realizing those prescriptions, or constrain their orders and classify every
group that can occur. The same kernel coordinates can organize both tasks. In the construction direction,
a local quotient is attached to a subset of the marker labels. In the classification direction, a canonical
nilpotent quotient confines the search to normal subgroups of one finite group.
The starting point is a vertex-and-edge model for a forest diagram. The intersection property forces
independent edge commutators in degree two, so every finite marked two-group with that diagram has
the same class-two quotient (§2). The affine square group supplies exact local kernels and two useful
quotient decompositions (§3). The supported-kernel theorem of the companion paper [U] then assembles
prescribed edge orders and consecutive square sections. Its coordinate-kernel Helly theorem upgrades
three dihedral attachments to an actual regular hypertope. A separate sharp order bound identifies the
source’s complete presentation (§4).
The reverse direction begins with a bounded cyclic core, reducing the high-type rank-three problem to
eight explicit models (§5). Central descent and complete binary lifting link the bounded quotients (§6).
One invariant-kernel census, followed by complete presentation and marked-isomorphism checks, yields
the order-1024 classification (§7). Finally, free geometric extensions use the amalgam theorem of [U]
and precisely stated earlier constructions (§8).
The six applications are the prescribed-order theorem of Hou, Feng and Leemans [HFLa], their high-
type rank-three classification [HFLb], their three-generator hypertope construction [HFLh], the common-
quotient theorem of Hou and Schulte [HS26], the square-section and extension theorems of Cunning-
ham, Feng, Hou and Schulte [CFHS25], and the order-1024 classification of Gomi, Loyola and De Las
Peñas [GLP18]. Their original results retain that attribution. The general algebraic proofs occur once
in [U]; this paper supplies the actual marked maps, local injectivity, geometric hypotheses and source-
presentation identifications needed to use them. Earlier cyclic-core, geometric-assembly and infinite-
decomposition theorems remain explicit retained inputs. All finite classification data and the complete
certificate interface are specified in Appendix C.
1. Markings, geometric inputs and the interface to U
A marking is an ordered generating family of nonidentity involutions 𝑟𝑖, indexed by a finite set 𝐼. Write
𝐺𝐽 = ⟨𝑟 𝑗 ∶ 𝑗 ∈ 𝐽 ⟩ . The intersection property is 𝐺𝐽 ∩ 𝐺 𝐾 = 𝐺 𝐽∩𝐾 for every pair of subsets. A
string C-group also has [𝑟𝑖, 𝑟𝑗] = 1 for |𝑖 − 𝑗| > 1 ; nondegeneracy means that consecutive generators
do not commute. The type is their consecutive product orders. A regular hypertope is a thin, residually
connected incidence geometry with a chamber-transitive type-preserving automorphism group. It need
not have a string diagram; its rank-three type here records all three pair-product orders. Use [𝑥, 𝑦] =
𝑥−1𝑦−1𝑥𝑦, 𝑥𝑦 = 𝑦 −1𝑥𝑦, and 𝐷8 for the dihedral group of order eight. Ordered marked isomorphisms
preserve every generator. The [GLP18] census instead identifies generator sets; for a nondegenerate
string this adds precisely reversal.
We retain the regular-polytope/string-C-group correspondence, the marked epimorphism/cover corre-
spondence and the coset description of faces and stabilizers. A deletion sends selected markers to them-
selves and all others to one. Its geometric interpretation gives the flat amalgamation property (FAP) at
the corresponding initial or final cut. Flatness itself is a separate incidence conclusion: a factorization of
the group by two relevant face stabilizers makes every face of the first kind incident with every face of
the second kind. It is never inferred merely from IP.

Proof. Every element of RL has a unique expression h(ab)ʲ with h∈R and 0≤j<m. Equality of two
