# Supported extensions and finite two-group quotients

## Abstract

We study marked groups through the intersections of subgroups generated by subsets of their markers. A construction over a group with deletion maps attaches local extensions whose kernels are invisible on proper local subgroups. We prove generation of the full compatible product, compute every parabolic kernel and obtain the intersection property. The same coordinates give local recognition, deletion maps, lower-central identities, separator factorizations and transfer of the flag property for coset geometries. For finite two-groups whose noncommuting diagram is a forest, we identify a canonical class-two quotient and a finite, exhaustive parameter space of marked quotients of any prescribed excess order. Explicit affine square groups supply local models of every required two-power order. The paper includes the supporting quotient, Frattini and amalgam arguments so that subsequent applications can cite complete statements with specified hypotheses.

## Introduction

A presentation by involutions does not by itself determine a regular polytope or hypertope. The distinguished generators must have the prescribed pair orders, their generated subgroups must intersect correctly, and a geometric conclusion may require a further common-representative condition on cosets. Establishing these properties separately for every presentation can obscure the part of the proof that is independent of its numerical parameters.

The main construction here retains an already understood marked quotient and records the extra relations in separate kernel coordinates. Each attached group is required to agree with the quotient on every proper part of its support. This hypothesis lets us isolate its whole kernel, even when one support contains another. Once the kernels have been isolated, all marked subgroup intersections and orders follow from a single coordinate calculation. The geometric assertion uses an additional proved transfer of typed coset Helly; it is not inferred from the intersection property alone.

A second branch identifies the quotient which every finite two-group marking on a forest must have. The proof uses the intersection property in the original group, a descending lower-central argument and the separation of a forest at an edge. It then reduces classification at any fixed excess order to normal subgroups of one finite nilpotent quotient. The explicit square groups in Section 7 provide a complementary construction: their orders vary, while their proper marked subgroups remain unchanged.

The immediate application in the companion paper *Prescribed rank, type and two-power order* is the existence theorem of Hou, Feng and Leemans [HFL20]. Other applications require additional arguments, including recognition of complete source presentations, bounds on cyclic cores, finite census computations or geometric assembly. Those arguments are not consequences of the words “fiber product” alone and are not claimed here.

This paper reorganizes existing complete proofs in the project's reviewed umbrella and two-group manuscript. It makes their hypotheses independent of that manuscript's section numbering. The original existence and classification results retain their source attribution. We make no claim of historical priority for the general arguments and no claim to solve an open conjecture.

## 1. Markings, images and kernels

A marked group is a group \(G\) together with a finite indexed generating family \((r_i)_{i\in I}\). Except where stated, the markers need not be involutions or distinct. For \(S\subseteq I\), put
\[
G_S=\langle r_i:i\in S\rangle,\qquad G_\varnothing=1.
\]
We call these subgroups parabolics, retaining their index sets even when two of them happen to coincide. The marking has the intersection property, or IP, if
\[
G_S\cap G_T=G_{S\cap T}\quad(S,T\subseteq I).
\tag{M1}
\]
A marked homomorphism sends each source marker to the corresponding target marker. Its image on \(G_S\) is therefore the target parabolic with index set \(S\).

All subgroup products below are products of sets unless a subgroup assertion is made. We write \(\gamma_1(G)=G\) and \(\gamma_{j+1}(G)=[\gamma_j(G),G]\). We use the elementary correspondence and isomorphism theorems for groups. In the finite \(p\)-group arguments we retain Burnside's basis theorem, the nilpotence of finite \(p\)-groups, and the usual multilinear commutator description of the lower-central graded Lie ring. Precisely, its degree-one part generates the graded Lie ring, and degree \(j\) is generated by left-normed commutators of weight \(j\) in any generating set. In Section 9 we explicitly retain the normal-form theorem for an amalgamated free product. None of these classical inputs is presented as a result originating in this manuscript.

**Lemma UA.K0 (lifting one intersection).** Let \(q:H\to Q\) be a homomorphism and let \(C\leq A\cap B\), where \(A,B,C\leq H\). If
\[
q(A)\cap q(B)=q(C),
\]
then
\[
A\cap B=C\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad A\cap B\cap\ker q\leq C.
\tag{M2}
\]

*Proof.* The forward implication is immediate. For the converse, let \(x\in A\cap B\). Its image belongs to \(q(C)\), so choose \(c\in C\) with \(q(c)=q(x)\). Then \(xc^{-1}\in A\cap B\cap\ker q\), which is contained in \(C\) by hypothesis. Multiplying by \(c\) gives \(x\in C\). The inclusion \(C\leq A\cap B\) gives equality. \(\square\)

**Theorem UA.K (kernel profiles).** Let \(q:H\twoheadrightarrow Q\) be a marked epimorphism and suppose that the marking of \(Q\) has IP. Write \(K=\ker q\) and \(K_S=K\cap H_S\). Then \(H\) has IP if and only if
\[
K_S\cap K_T=K_{S\cap T}\quad(S,T\subseteq I).
\tag{M3}
\]

*Proof.* Apply UA.K0 to \(A=H_S\), \(B=H_T\), and \(C=H_{S\cap T}\). The image hypothesis is exactly IP in \(Q\). The subgroup on the left of the containment in (M2) is \(K_S\cap K_T\). Its containment in \(H_{S\cap T}\) is equivalent to its containment in \(K_{S\cap T}\); the reverse inclusion always holds. This proves each required intersection and its converse. \(\square\)

The next result concerns the opposite direction. Passing to a quotient can create new intersections, and this defect is a product condition in its kernel.

**Theorem UA.Q (exact intersections of images).** Let \(q:H\to Q\) be a homomorphism with kernel \(N\), and let \(A,B\leq H\). Then
\[
q(A)\cap q(B)=q(A\cap B)
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
N\cap AB=(N\cap A)(N\cap B).
\tag{M4}
\]
If \(q\) is injective on \(A\) and \(B\), the right side reduces to \(N\cap AB=\{1\}\). If \(N\leq A\), the equality always holds.

*Proof.* Suppose the image intersection is exact, and take \(ab\in N\), where \(a\in A\) and \(b\in B\). Since \(q(a)=q(b)^{-1}\), choose \(c\in A\cap B\) with this image. Then
\[
ab=(ac^{-1})(cb)\in(N\cap A)(N\cap B).
\]
The reverse inclusion requires no hypothesis. Conversely, if \(q(a)=q(b)\), then \(ab^{-1}\in N\cap AB\). Write \(ab^{-1}=uv\), with \(u\in N\cap A\) and \(v\in N\cap B\). The element \(u^{-1}a=vb\) lies in \(A\cap B\) and has image \(q(a)\), proving the required image equality. Under injectivity both factors on the right of (M4) are trivial. If \(N\leq A\), both sides of that equality are \(N\). \(\square\)

## 2. Deletion maps and supported extensions

A deletion for \(S\subseteq I\) is an endomorphism \(e_S:G\to G\) satisfying \(e_S(r_i)=r_i\) for \(i\in S\) and \(e_S(r_i)=1\) otherwise. Requiring these values matters: a general retraction onto \(G_S\) need not be a deletion.

**Proposition UA.D (Boolean deletions).** If a marked group has a deletion \(e_S\) for every \(S\subseteq I\), then
\[
e_Se_T=e_{S\cap T},\qquad \operatorname{im}e_S=\operatorname{Fix}(e_S)=G_S.
\tag{D1}
\]
The marking has IP, and
\[
\ker e_S=\langle\!\langle r_i:i\notin S\rangle\!\rangle_G,
\qquad G=(\ker e_S)\rtimes G_S.
\tag{D2}
\]

*Proof.* The two maps in the first equality agree on every generator. The image is generated by precisely the retained markers, and each of those markers is fixed; this proves the image and fixed-point statements. An element of \(G_S\cap G_T\) is fixed by \(e_Se_T=e_{S\cap T}\), so belongs to \(G_{S\cap T}\). The reverse inclusion is immediate.

Let \(M\) be the normal closure in (D2). Clearly \(M\leq\ker e_S\). In \(G/M\), every omitted marker is trivial, so the image of any word equals the image of its deletion. A word in \(\ker e_S\) is therefore trivial in \(G/M\), proving equality. Finally, for \(g\in G\), the factorization \(g=(g e_S(g)^{-1})e_S(g)\) has factors in \(\ker e_S\) and \(G_S\). Their intersection is trivial because \(e_S\) fixes \(G_S\). This is the asserted semidirect product. \(\square\)

We now assemble extensions whose extra kernel is invisible on each proper part of its support. The groups and kernels may be infinite and nonabelian.

**Theorem UA.SK (supported-kernel realization).** Let \(I\) be finite and let \(\mathcal F\) be a finite family of distinct subsets of \(I\). Let \(B=\langle b_i:i\in I\rangle\) have IP. For every \(S\in\mathcal F\), suppose the following data are given:

1. A deletion homomorphism \(p_S:B\to B_S\), fixing \(b_i\) for \(i\in S\) and killing \(b_i\) otherwise.
2. A group \(E_S=\langle e_{S,i}:i\in S\rangle\) and a marked epimorphism \(q_S:E_S\to B_S\).
3. Injectivity of \(q_S\) on \((E_S)_T=\langle e_{S,i}:i\in T\rangle\) for every \(T\subsetneq S\).

Put \(N_S=\ker q_S\), and form the group
\[
H=\left\{(b,(x_S)_{S\in\mathcal F})\in
B\times\prod_{S\in\mathcal F}E_S:
q_S(x_S)=p_S(b)\text{ for every }S\right\}.
\tag{K1}
\]
For \(i\in I\), define \(h_i\) to have base coordinate \(b_i\) and \(S\)-coordinate \(e_{S,i}\) when \(i\in S\), and \(1\) otherwise. These elements generate all of \(H\). If \(\pi:H\to B\) is the base projection and \(H_L=\langle h_i:i\in L\rangle\), then, for every \(L\subseteq I\),
\[
\pi(H_L)=B_L,\qquad
\ker\pi=\prod_{S\in\mathcal F}N_S,\qquad
\ker\pi\cap H_L=\prod_{\substack{S\in\mathcal F\\S\subseteq L}}N_S.
\tag{K2}
\]
The factors on the right are embedded as pure coordinate subgroups. The marking of \(H\) has IP. If \(B\) and all \(E_S\) are finite, then
\[
|H_L|=|B_L|\prod_{\substack{S\in\mathcal F\\S\subseteq L}}|N_S|.
\tag{K3}
\]

*Proof.* The compatibility equations in (K1) are preserved by products and inverses, so \(H\) is a subgroup of the displayed direct product. The prescribed tuple \(h_i\) satisfies those equations by the literal deletion rule. Let \(H'\) be the subgroup generated by these tuples. Its base image is \(B\), and the projection of \(H'_S=\langle h_i:i\in S\rangle\) onto \(E_S\) is onto.

We first prove, by induction on inclusion among the finitely many supports, that every pure coordinate element of \(N_S\) belongs to \(H'_S\). Fix \(n\in N_S\). Choose a word on the markers indexed by \(S\) which evaluates to \(n\) in \(E_S\), and evaluate the same word in \(H'\), obtaining \(w\in H'_S\). Its base coordinate lies in \(B_S\) and is \(q_S(n)=1\).

Consider its coordinate at another support \(T\). Because the word uses only markers from \(S\), that coordinate lies in \((E_T)_{S\cap T}\). Its image under \(q_T\) is \(p_T(1)=1\). If \(T\not\subseteq S\), then \(S\cap T\subsetneq T\), and the proper-parabolic injectivity hypothesis forces this coordinate to be \(1\). The only possible nonidentity coordinates of \(w\) are consequently at supports \(T\subseteq S\). Its \(S\)-coordinate is the prescribed \(n\), while every coordinate at a proper support \(T\subsetneq S\) belongs to \(N_T\).

For each such proper support, the induction hypothesis supplies the pure coordinate element having exactly that value in \(N_T\), as an element of \(H'_T\leq H'_S\). Multiply \(w\) by the inverses of those elements. Distinct pure coordinate subgroups commute in the ambient direct product, so these operations remove the unwanted coordinates without changing the \(S\)-coordinate. The result is the pure element \(n\) in coordinate \(S\), and remains in \(H'_S\). A minimal support has no smaller coordinates to remove, so this argument includes the initial step. No commutativity within an individual kernel has been used.

The kernel of \(\pi\) in the full group \(H\) is exactly the direct product of the \(N_S\), by (K1). The induction places that entire kernel in \(H'\). Since \(H'\) already maps onto \(B\), every \(h\in H\) has the same base coordinate as some \(h'\in H'\); then \(h(h')^{-1}\in\ker\pi\leq H'\). Thus \(H'=H\).

The image equality \(\pi(H_L)=B_L\) follows on generators. If \(x\in H_L\cap\ker\pi\), its \(S\)-coordinate lies in \((E_S)_{L\cap S}\) and in \(N_S\). This coordinate is trivial whenever \(S\not\subseteq L\), again by proper-parabolic injectivity. Conversely, when \(S\subseteq L\), the isolation argument placed the entire pure \(N_S\) in \(H_S\leq H_L\). This proves all three assertions in (K2).

The coordinate products in (K2) satisfy
\[
\left(\prod_{S\subseteq L}N_S\right)\cap
\left(\prod_{S\subseteq M}N_S\right)
=\prod_{S\subseteq L\cap M}N_S.
\]
Theorem UA.K now proves IP in \(H\). More explicitly, an element of \(H_L\cap H_M\) has base in \(B_{L\cap M}\); subtracting a lift from \(H_{L\cap M}\) leaves an element in both displayed kernel products, hence in the kernel product for \(L\cap M\). This also shows exactly where IP of the base is used. Finally (K3) is the order formula for the surjection \(H_L\to B_L\) with the kernel just computed. \(\square\)

**Corollary UA.SK.Local (recognition on an antichain).** Under UA.SK, if \(\mathcal F\) is an antichain under inclusion, then the coordinate projection \(H_S\to E_S\) is a marked isomorphism for every \(S\in\mathcal F\).

*Proof.* It is onto on generators. If an element maps to \(1\), its base coordinate also vanishes, since the base value of a word on \(S\) is the \(q_S\)-image of its \(S\)-coordinate. Formula (K2) says that the remaining kernel in \(H_S\) has only the factor \(N_S\): the antichain excludes every other support contained in \(S\). The \(S\)-coordinate detects that factor, so the element is \(1\). \(\square\)

**Corollary UA.SK.Delete (derived deletions).** If, in addition, \(B\) has deletions for every subset of \(I\), then every \(E_S\) and \(H\) has all its Boolean deletions.

*Proof.* For \(T\subsetneq S\), the restriction \(q_S:(E_S)_T\to B_T\) is a marked isomorphism: it is onto on generators and injective by hypothesis. Compose \(q_S\), the deletion \(B_S\to B_T\), and the inverse of this isomorphism. This gives a homomorphism \(E_S\to(E_S)_T\) that fixes exactly the \(T\)-markers and kills the others. For \(T=S\) use the identity. Proposition UA.D supplies the Boolean identities.

For \(L\subseteq I\), apply the base deletion \(e_L\) and, in coordinate \(S\), the local deletion onto \(S\cap L\). The two sides of each compatibility equation agree on the local markers; the base deletions commute by UA.D. Thus the coordinatewise operation preserves (K1). On \(h_i\) it fixes \(h_i\) if \(i\in L\), and kills it otherwise. It is the required deletion of \(H\). \(\square\)

**Corollary UA.SK.Central (lower-central kernel).** Under UA.SK, suppose \(c\geq1\), \(\gamma_c(B)=1\), and \(N_S=\gamma_c(E_S)\) for every support. Then
\[
\gamma_c(H)=\ker\pi.
\tag{K4}
\]

*Proof.* Applying \(\pi\) to a commutator of weight \(c\) gives \(1\), so \(\gamma_c(H)\leq\ker\pi\). For the converse, repeat the inclusion induction in the proof of UA.SK. The projection \(H_S\to E_S\) is onto; therefore its image on \(\gamma_c(H_S)\) is \(\gamma_c(E_S)=N_S\). Given \(n\in N_S\), choose a lift \(w\in\gamma_c(H_S)\). Its base is \(1\), since \(\gamma_c(B)=1\). The same proper-support argument makes all coordinates at \(T\not\subseteq S\) trivial. The remaining proper-support coordinates lie in \(N_T\); by induction their pure copies belong to \(\gamma_c(H)\). Removing them leaves the pure \(n\), still in \(\gamma_c(H)\). All pure kernels are obtained, proving (K4). \(\square\)

**Corollary UA.SK.Separator (factorization across a separator).** Under UA.SK, let \(I=L\cup M\). Suppose \(B=B_LB_M\) as a product of sets, and every support in \(\mathcal F\) is contained in \(L\) or in \(M\). Then \(H=H_LH_M\).

*Proof.* For \(h\in H\), factor \(\pi(h)=b_Lb_M\) and choose lifts \(u_L\in H_L\), \(u_M\in H_M\). Then \(h=n u_Lu_M\) for some \(n\in\ker\pi\). Assign every pure coordinate factor of \(n\) to one of the two sides containing its support. Formula (K2) gives \(n=n_Ln_M\), with \(n_L\in H_L\), \(n_M\in H_M\). Each pure \(N_S\) is normal in \(H\), because conjugation in the ambient product acts coordinatewise and \(N_S\unlhd E_S\). Hence the product of the assigned \(M\)-factors is normal in \(H\). Moving \(n_M\) past \(u_L\) gives
\[
h=(n_Lu_L)(u_L^{-1}n_Mu_L\,u_M)\in H_LH_M.
\]
The opposite inclusion is automatic. \(\square\)

The hypotheses of UA.SK do not identify \(H\) with a universal amalgam of the \(E_S\). Additional cross-relations and a presentation-recognition argument may be necessary. Nor can injectivity on every proper parabolic be replaced by injectivity on individual markers alone.

**Proposition UA.Fiber (two compatible split factors).** Let \(A\) and \(B\) be marked groups on finite label sets \(L\) and \(M\), and put \(J=L\cap M\). Suppose their \(J\)-parabolics are identified with the same marked group \(C\), and that \(p_A:A\to C\) and \(p_B:B\to C\) are deletions. Then
\[
A\times_C B\cong(\ker p_A\times\ker p_B)\rtimes C.
\tag{F1}
\]
This full fiber product is generated by the tuples \((a_i,1)\) for \(i\in L\setminus J\), \((1,b_i)\) for \(i\in M\setminus J\), and \((c_i,c_i)\) for \(i\in J\). The two original groups embed through \(a\mapsto(a,p_A(a))\) and \(b\mapsto(p_B(b),b)\). If finite, its order is \(|A||B|/|C|\).

*Proof.* Every compatible pair has a unique form \((n_Ac,n_Bc)\), giving the split decomposition and the order formula. The stated copies of \(A\) and \(B\) are generated by subsets of the stated tuple marking. They contain \((n_A,1)\) and \((1,n_B)\), respectively, while either contains the diagonal copy of \(C\). These subgroups generate the split product, proving the assertion. The embedding formulas are injective because their first and second projections, respectively, are identities. \(\square\)

## 3. Coordinate kernels and incidence geometries

An incidence system has a finite type set, objects carrying types, and a symmetric incidence relation; distinct objects of one type are not incident. A flag is a set of pairwise incident objects. A chamber has one object of each type. A geometry is an incidence system in which every flag extends to a chamber. The residue of a flag consists of the objects of the remaining types incident with that flag, with induced incidence. The geometry is residually connected when the incidence graph of every residue of rank at least two, including the empty-flag residue, is connected. It is thin when every panel, a flag missing just one type, lies in exactly two chambers. A regular hypertope is a thin, residually connected geometry admitting a chamber-transitive type-preserving automorphism group.

For a marked group indexed by \(I\), its maximal parabolics are \(P_i=G_{I\setminus\{i\}}\). The associated typed coset incidence system has objects \(P_i g\), and incidence means nonempty intersection of cosets. All actions and cosets below use this right-coset convention.

For subgroups \(P_i\leq G\), typed coset Helly means that every pairwise intersecting family of right cosets \(P_i g_i\), with distinct indices, has a common element. This property is separate from IP. It supplies a common representative for an incident flag, whereas IP determines the stabilizer of a flag already represented in this way.

**Proposition UA.Geometry (coset recognition and faithful chamber action).** Suppose the marking of \(G\) consists of nonidentity involutions, has IP, and has typed coset Helly for its maximal parabolics. The coset incidence system is then a thin, residually connected geometry, and \(G\) is its full type-preserving automorphism group, acting regularly on chambers. More generally, the full type-preserving automorphism group of any thin, residually connected geometry of finite rank acts freely on chambers. A chamber-transitive subgroup is consequently the full group.

*Proof.* In the coset system, a common coset point extends every flag to a chamber by choosing the coset of each missing type through that point. Right translation sends every such flag to its base flag. For a base flag of type set \(S\), IP gives stabilizer \(G_{I\setminus S}\). In particular the full chamber stabilizer is \(1\), while a panel stabilizer is a group generated by one nonidentity involution and has order two. There are therefore exactly two chambers through each panel.

Consider a residue with remaining type set \(T\), where \(|T|\geq2\). Its stabilizer is \(G_T\), and its maximal object stabilizers are \(G_{T\setminus\{i\}}\), \(i\in T\). Every marker indexed by \(T\) lies in at least one of these proper parabolics. A word in those subgroups supplies a walk between their translated base objects in the residue incidence graph; all objects are translates of base objects and all base objects lie in a common flag. The residue is therefore connected.

For the general assertion, first observe that every residually connected finite-rank geometry has a connected chamber graph. This follows by induction on rank: chambers through a fixed object are connected through galleries in its residue, and a path in the incidence graph, extending each consecutive incident pair to a chamber, connects arbitrary chambers through these subgraphs. The rank-one case holds because all chambers share the empty panel. In a thin geometry an automorphism fixing a chamber fixes its unique neighbor across each panel. It therefore fixes every chamber along every gallery, and hence every object. The chamber action is free. If a subgroup is chamber-transitive, any full automorphism agrees on a base chamber with one subgroup element; their quotient fixes that chamber and is the identity. Apply this to the coset geometry to obtain the stated full automorphism group. \(\square\)

### 3.1. Helly transfer through coordinate kernels

Write right cosets as \(P g\), with the subgroup on the left. Let \(q:H\to B\) be an epimorphism of groups generated by corresponding marked families \((h_i)_{i\in I}\) and \((b_i)_{i\in I}\), where \(I\) is finite. For \(J\subseteq I\), write \(H_J=\langle h_j:j\in J\rangle\) and \(B_J=\langle b_j:j\in J\rangle\). In particular, \(q(H_J)=B_J\) for every J.

Fix any set \(\mathcal J\) of subsets of I as the permitted types. The maximal-parabolic case is \(\mathcal J=\{I\setminus\{i\}:i\in I\}\).

**Theorem UA.SK.Helly (coordinate-kernel transfer).** Suppose B has IP. Suppose \(N=\ker q\) is an internal direct product \(\prod_{\alpha\in A}N_\alpha\), with a finite factor set A, and for every \(J\in\mathcal J\),
\[
 N\cap H_J=\prod_{\alpha\in A_J}N_\alpha
 \qquad\text{for some }A_J\subseteq A.
 \tag{H1}
\]
If the family \((B_J)_{J\in\mathcal J}\) has typed coset Helly, then so does \((H_J)_{J\in\mathcal J}\).

No commutativity within the factors, finiteness of H or B, or faithfulness of an action of B on N is assumed. The required intersection-image fact is
\(q(H_{J\cap K})=B_J\cap B_K\); it follows here from the marked map and IP of B. IP of H is not an additional assumption for this transfer theorem.

*Proof.* Consider pairwise intersecting cosets \(H_{J_i}g_i\). Their images \(B_{J_i}q(g_i)\) intersect pairwise. Choose a common base element b by the assumed Helly property, and choose u in H with \(q(u)=b\). Each set
\[
 F_i=H_{J_i}g_i\cap q^{-1}(b)
\]
is nonempty: because \(bq(g_i)^{-1}\in B_{J_i}\), a preimage of that element in \(H_{J_i}\), multiplied on the right by \(g_i\), belongs to \(F_i\).

We first show that \(F_i\cap F_j\ne\varnothing\). Choose \(x\in H_{J_i}g_i\cap H_{J_j}g_j\) and put z=q(x). Both b and z belong to each of the two base cosets, so
\[
 bz^{-1}\in B_{J_i}\cap B_{J_j}=B_{J_i\cap J_j}.
\]
The marked map is surjective on this intersection parabolic. Choose \(h\in H_{J_i\cap J_j}\) with \(q(h)=bz^{-1}\). Since h belongs to both \(H_{J_i}\) and \(H_{J_j}\), left multiplication by h preserves both right cosets. Thus hx belongs to their intersection and has image b. This proves the assertion without assuming that q carries an arbitrary intersection onto the intersection of its images.

Choose \(x_i\in F_i\) and put \(k_i=x_i u^{-1}\in N\). If \(y\in F_i\), then \(yx_i^{-1}\in N\cap H_{J_i}\); the converse also holds. Hence
\[
 F_i u^{-1}=(N\cap H_{J_i})k_i.
 \tag{H2}
\]
Under the direct product coordinates of N, (H1) makes (H2) unrestricted in coordinates \(\alpha\in A_{J_i}\), and fixes coordinate \(\alpha\notin A_{J_i}\) to \((k_i)_\alpha\). Pairwise intersections imply that any two constraints fixing the same coordinate fix it to the same value. Choose that value in every constrained coordinate and the identity in every unconstrained coordinate. The resulting element belongs to all sets in (H2). Multiplication on the right by u supplies a common element of the original cosets. This proves the theorem. \(\square\)

**Corollary UA.SK.Geometry.** In the supported-kernel realization UA.SK, assume the base marking gives a regular hypertope and every marked generator in every attached group is an involution. Then the resulting marking also gives a regular hypertope, and its full type-preserving automorphism group is the constructed group.

*Proof.* UA.SK gives IP and the exact profile
\[
 N\cap H_J=\prod_{S\subseteq J}N_S.
\]
The base maximal-parabolic cosets have typed Helly. Theorem UA.SK.Helly applies with factors indexed by the supports S. Each constructed generator is a tuple whose base coordinate and every nontrivial attached coordinate are involutions; hence its square is the identity. Its base image is nonidentity, so its order is exactly two. UA.Geometry now gives thinness, residual connectedness, chamber regularity and equality with the full type-preserving automorphism group. In particular, this conclusion uses typed Helly in addition to IP. \(\square\)

A sufficient alternative to local involutivity is that every support has size at least two: each singleton local parabolic is then proper, and injectivity into its nonidentity involutory base image forces the local marker to have order two. This is a sufficient condition, not an equivalent reformulation. Without either condition the geometric corollary fails. With a singleton type set and support, the quotient \(C_4\to C_2\) is injective on its only proper parabolic, the trivial subgroup. UA.SK then constructs \(C_4\), whose distinguished generator has order four. In rank three, adjoining two independent \(C_2\) markers gives \(C_4\times C_2^2\) with the same defective singleton attachment and a four-element panel.

## 4. Frattini coordinates

**Theorem UA.Phi (primitive saturation and coordinates).** Let \(P\) be a finite \(p\)-group with an irredundant generating marking \((r_i)_{i\in I}\), \(|I|=d\), and let \(F=\Phi(P)\). Then, for every \(S\subseteq I\),
\[
\Phi(P_S)=P_S\cap F,\qquad
P_SF=\pi^{-1}\operatorname{span}(\bar r_i:i\in S),\qquad
[P:P_SF]=p^{d-|S|}.
\tag{P1}
\]
The saturated parabolics \(P_SF\) intersect according to intersection of index sets. Each omission subgroup \(P_{I\setminus\{i\}}\) has exactly one maximal overgroup, namely \(P_{I\setminus\{i\}}F\).

If \(B\geq F\) and \((s_i)_{i\in J}\) is an irredundant relative generating family over \(B\), then \(B\unlhd P\), \(P/B\) has basis \((s_iB)_{i\in J}\) over \(\mathbb F_p\), and
\[
\langle B,s_i:i\in T\rangle/B=\operatorname{span}(s_iB:i\in T).
\tag{P2}
\]
If \(p=2\) and all original \(r_i\) are involutions, then \(F=P'\) and \(\Phi(P_S)=P_S'\) for every \(S\).

*Proof.* Burnside's basis theorem makes the \(\bar r_i\) a basis of \(P/F\). The image of \(P_S\) therefore has order \(p^{|S|}\), with kernel \(P_S\cap F\). Since this image is elementary abelian, \(\Phi(P_S)\leq P_S\cap F\). On the other hand \(P_S\) is generated by \(|S|\) elements, so Burnside gives \([P_S:\Phi(P_S)]\leq p^{|S|}\). The quotient by \(P_S\cap F\) already has that order. Equality follows, proving the first formula. The remaining formulas and the intersection assertion are inverse-image statements for coordinate subspaces.

Every maximal subgroup of a finite \(p\)-group contains \(F\) and corresponds to a hyperplane of \(P/F\). A hyperplane containing a specified coordinate hyperplane equals it, proving uniqueness of the maximal overgroup of an omission subgroup.

For the relative assertion, every subgroup containing \(F\) is normal, because \(P/F\) is abelian. The quotient \(P/B\) is elementary abelian. The indicated images span it, and any nontrivial linear relation would express one \(s_i\) using \(B\) and the other markers, contradicting irredundancy. They form a basis, proving (P2). Finally, an abelian group generated by involutions is elementary abelian of exponent two. Thus \(P'\leq\Phi(P)\), while the elementary abelian quotient \(P/P'\) gives the reverse inclusion. The same argument applies to each \(P_S\). \(\square\)

These coordinates also describe the corresponding omission cosets: a coset prescribes one coordinate value, and every simultaneous assignment gives a chamber. The geometry is complete multipartite, with \(p\) objects in each type, and the quotient translation group acts regularly on chambers. For \(p>2\) it need not be the full type-preserving automorphism group; the thin case \(p=2\) is the one covered by UA.Geometry.

## 5. The canonical quotient of a forest marking

Suppose a group is generated by nonidentity involutions \(r_i\), \(i\in I\). Its noncommutation graph has vertex set \(I\), and an edge \(\{i,j\}\) precisely when \(r_i\) and \(r_j\) do not commute. We write \(G_J=\langle r_j:j\in J\rangle\), as in Section 1. The intersection property in this section always refers to the original group. It is not assumed to survive an intermediate quotient.

**Lemma UA.Lift (descending lift).** Let \(G\) be a finite nilpotent group, let \(A,B\le G\) satisfy
\(A\cap B=1\), and fix an integer \(m\ge1\). Put \(D_j=\gamma_j(G)\), \(A_j=A\cap D_j\),
\(B_j=B\cap D_j\). If the images of \(A_j,B_j\) generate
\(D_j/D_{j+1}\) for every \(j\ge m\), then \(D_j=A_jB_j\) for
every \(j\ge m\).

**Proof.** Descend from a trivial term. Assuming
\(D_{j+1}=A_{j+1}B_{j+1}\), if \(a\in A_j,b\in B_j\) have equal
images in the next quotient, write \(ab^{-1}=a'b'\) with
\(a'\in A_{j+1},b'\in B_{j+1}\). Then \(a'^{-1}a=b'b\in A\cap B\),
so both images are trivial. Their images therefore form an internal
direct sum in the abelian quotient. Counting gives
\(|D_j|=|A_j||B_j|=|A_jB_j|\), proving equality as sets. No prior
normality or permutability of \(A,B\) is assumed. ∎

**Lemma UA.Support (graded support).** Let a positively graded Lie algebra be
generated in degree one by disjoint sets \(X,Y\), with subalgebras
\(L_X,L_Y\). If \([x,y]\in L_Y\) for \(x\in X,y\in Y\), then
\(L=L_X+L_Y\), degree by degree.

**Proof.** The derivation \(\operatorname{ad}x\) preserves \(L_Y\)
because it does so on its generators. Iterated brackets of those
derivations also preserve \(L_Y\), by Jacobi. Hence
\([L_X,L_Y]\subseteq L_Y\), and \(L_X+L_Y\) is a Lie subalgebra
containing all generators. The argument holds in characteristic two. ∎

**Theorem UA.Forest (forest quotient).** Suppose \(G\) is a finite two-group generated by nonidentity involutions \(r_i\), with marked IP, and its noncommuting pairs form a forest \(T\) on
\(d\) vertices and \(m\) edges. Then edge commutators are independent
in \(G'/\gamma_3(G)\), and \(G/\gamma_3(G)\) is the marked group
\[
 (v,z)(w,t)=(v+w,z+t+\beta(v,w)),\quad
 v,w\in\mathbb F_2^d,\ z,t\in\mathbb F_2^m,               \tag{F0}
\]
where, after orienting each edge, its component of \(\beta\) is
\(v_{\rm head}w_{\rm tail}\). Its order is \(2^{d+m}\).

**Proof.** IP gives irredundancy. UA.Phi gives \(G/G'=C_2^d\) and \(G'\cap\langle r_i\rangle=1\).
The lower-central graded Lie ring is generated by these degree-one
elements; all its graded components are \(\mathbb F_2\)-spaces by
bilinearity. Edge commutators span degree two. If they satisfy a nonzero
linear relation, root each forest component and choose a participating
edge whose child endpoint has maximal depth in its component. Removing
it separates the descendant set \(X\) from all other vertices \(Y\),
and no other participating edge is internal to \(X\). The relation
therefore writes the only nonzero cross bracket as a sum of brackets
internal to \(Y\). Lemma UA.Support shows every graded component is generated
by pure \(X\)- and pure \(Y\)-brackets. Their representatives lie in
\(G_X\cap\gamma_j(G)\) and \(G_Y\cap\gamma_j(G)\).

Actual IP gives \(G_X\cap G_Y=1\), so Lemma UA.Lift gives
\(G'=(G'\cap G_X)(G'\cap G_Y)\). For the crossing endpoints
\(x\in X,y\in Y\), write \([r_x,r_y]=uv\) accordingly. Then
\(u\in G_X\cap G_{\{x\}\cup Y}=\langle r_x\rangle\), whence
\(u=1\); next \([r_x,r_y]\in G_Y\cap G_{\{x,y\}}=\langle r_y\rangle\),
so it too is one. This contradicts that the edge is noncommuting.
Independence follows.

Bilinearity makes (F0) associative; its coordinate markers are
involutions with independent central edge commutators. It is generated
by those markers, and its parabolic on any subset \(J\) consists exactly of vertex
coordinates supported on \(J\) and edge coordinates supported on edges
internal to \(J\). Thus it has IP. Restricting the vertex coordinates to a subset and retaining exactly its internal edge coordinates preserves the multiplication formula, so it also gives every Boolean deletion. Collection in the corresponding
class-two presentation gives at most \(2^{d+m}\) elements, and this
coordinate model attains the bound. Independence in \(G'/\gamma_3(G)\)
gives the same order for \(G/\gamma_3(G)\); hence the marked map is an
isomorphism. ∎

## 6. Finite quotients of prescribed excess order

The next theorem separates a universal finite search space from the task of proving that a particular family has the required class-two quotient. That identification is an application hypothesis, not a consequence of the search.

**Theorem UA.Nilpotent (complete marked kernel parameter space).** Let \(F=\langle x_i:i\in I\rangle\), where \(|I|=d\) and \(x_i^2=1\). Suppose \(F\) has all Boolean deletions. Put \(Q=F/\gamma_3(F)\), with its image marking. For \(k\geq0\), set
\[
U=F/\gamma_{k+3}(F),\qquad K=\gamma_3(U),\qquad
K_S=K\cap U_S.
\tag{N1}
\]
Then \(U\) is a finite two-group,
\[
|U|\leq 2^{\,\sum_{j=1}^{k+2}d^j},
\tag{N2}
\]
the markings of \(U\) and \(Q\) have Boolean deletions and IP, and \(U/K\) is canonically the marked group \(Q\). For every \(S\subseteq I\),
\[
K_S=\gamma_3(U_S).
\tag{N3}
\]

There is a bijection between the following two sets:

1. Ordered marked isomorphism classes of finite two-group quotients \(f:F\twoheadrightarrow G\) for which the induced marked map \(Q\to G/\gamma_3(G)\) is an isomorphism, \(|G|=|Q|2^k\), and \(G\) has IP.
2. Normal subgroups \(N\unlhd U\) satisfying \(N\leq K\), \([K:N]=2^k\), and
\[
\frac{NK_S}{N}\cap\frac{NK_T}{N}
=\frac{NK_{S\cap T}}{N}
\quad(S,T\subseteq I).
\tag{N4}
\]

The correspondence sends \(N\) to \(U/N\) with the original image markers. If \(D\leq\operatorname{Sym}(I)\) is specified and each \(\sigma\in D\) induces an automorphism \(x_i\mapsto x_{\sigma(i)}\) of \(F\), then equivalence allowing precisely these label permutations is the \(D\)-orbit relation on the kernels in item 2.

*Proof.* The degree-one part of the lower-central graded Lie ring of \(F\) is generated by elements of exponent dividing two. By multilinearity, every iterated commutator in those generators also has exponent dividing two in its homogeneous component. In degree \(j\), at most \(d^j\) left-normed commutators suffice to generate that component. Consequently
\[
|\gamma_j(F)/\gamma_{j+1}(F)|\leq2^{d^j}.
\]
Multiplying these bounds for \(1\leq j\leq k+2\) proves finiteness, the two-power order and (N2). Each deletion preserves every lower-central term, and therefore descends both to \(U\) and to \(Q\). Proposition UA.D gives IP for both image markings; no separate IP hypothesis on \(Q\) is needed. The epimorphic-image property of the lower central series gives \(U/K\cong F/\gamma_3(F)=Q\).

For (N3), one inclusion follows from \(U_S\leq U\). If \(z\in\gamma_3(U)\cap U_S\), the deletion \(e_S\) fixes \(z\) and sends \(\gamma_3(U)\) into \(\gamma_3(U_S)\). This proves the reverse inclusion.

Let \(G\) satisfy item 1. Its subgroup \(\gamma_3(G)\) has order \(2^k\). In a nilpotent group, equality between two successive nontrivial lower-central terms would persist at every later term, contradicting termination of that series. Thus each nontrivial step beginning at \(\gamma_3(G)\) strictly decreases its order. There are at most \(k\) such steps, and \(\gamma_{k+3}(G)=1\). The map \(F\to G\) therefore factors through \(U\). Its kernel \(N\) lies in \(K\), since the induced map \(U/K\to G/\gamma_3(G)\) is an isomorphism, and the orders give \([K:N]=2^k\).

Conversely, let \(N\unlhd U\) with \(N\leq K\) and \([K:N]=2^k\), and write \(G=U/N\). Its third lower-central subgroup is \(K/N\), its canonical class-two quotient is \(Q\), and its order is \(|Q|2^k\). If \(q:U\to G\), then
\[
(K/N)\cap G_S=q(K\cap U_S)=NK_S/N.
\tag{N5}
\]
Indeed, \(q(u)\in K/N\) with \(u\in U_S\) implies \(u\in K\), because \(q^{-1}(K/N)=K\). The reverse inclusion is immediate. Applying UA.K to \(G\to Q\) proves that IP in \(G\) is equivalent to precisely (N4).

An isomorphism preserving each ordered marker commutes with the two maps from \(U\), because it agrees with them on a generating family. Their kernels are consequently equal. Equal kernels plainly give the same marked quotient. Finally, every specified label permutation acts on \(F\), preserves its characteristic lower-central terms, and hence acts on \(U\) and on the displayed kernels. A marker-permuting isomorphism induces the corresponding kernel equality after this automorphism, and the converse descends that automorphism to the quotients. This proves the orbit assertion at exactly the specified equivalence scope. \(\square\)

**Theorem UA.Ladder (invariant index-two descent).** Let \(U\) be a finite two-group and \(M\unlhd U\). The \(U\)-normal subgroups \(L\leq M\) of index two are exactly the inverse images of the hyperplanes of
\[
M/\bigl(\Phi(M)[M,U]\bigr).
\tag{N6}
\]
For \(K\unlhd U\), every \(U\)-normal subgroup \(N\leq K\) of index \(2^k\) is obtained by \(k\) successive such steps.

*Proof.* An index-two subgroup \(L\) of \(M\) contains \(\Phi(M)\) and has quotient \(C_2\). If \(L\) is \(U\)-normal, conjugation by \(U\) on \(M/L\) is trivial, since \(\operatorname{Aut}(C_2)=1\). Thus \([M,U]\leq L\). Conversely, a hyperplane inverse image has index two and contains \([M,U]\); for \(l\in L\) and \(u\in U\), the conjugate of \(l\) differs from \(l\) by an element of \([M,U]\), so lies in \(L\). The quotient in (N6) is elementary abelian by Burnside's basis theorem, proving the first assertion.

Suppose \(N\unlhd U\) and \(N<M\). The nontrivial finite two-group \(M/N\) has a nonzero Frattini quotient \(V\). Conjugation by \(U/N\) acts on the nonzero linear maps \(V\to\mathbb F_2\). If \(\dim V=a\), there are \(2^a-1\) such maps, an odd number. Every orbit has two-power size, so at least one orbit has size one. The kernel of a fixed nonzero map lifts to a \(U\)-normal subgroup between \(N\) and \(M\), of index two in \(M\). Repeating with the new subgroup gives a chain terminating at \(N\); its length is \(k\), since the total index is \(2^k\). \(\square\)

**Lemma UA.Order (presentation recognition by a sharp bound).** Let \(A_1,\ldots,A_t\) be finite normal subgroups of \(G\), with \(|A_j|\leq a_j\). Suppose \([G:A_1\cdots A_t]\leq b\). Then \(|G|\leq b\prod_j a_j\). If a finite quotient of \(G\) has this latter order, its quotient map is an isomorphism.

*Proof.* Normality makes each partial product a subgroup. The multiplication map from \(A_1\times\cdots\times A_t\) onto the product is surjective as a map of sets, so that product has order at most \(\prod_j a_j\). Multiplication by the quotient index proves the bound and finiteness of \(G\). A quotient attaining the bound forces equality of the finite orders and hence a trivial kernel. \(\square\)

**Corollary UA.Search (rank and excess classify the finite search).** Let T be a
specified forest on d vertices and m edges, and let k≥0. Set

\[
 F_T=\langle x_i\mid x_i^2=1,\ [x_i,x_j]=1\text{ for nonedges}\rangle,
 \quad U_{T,k}=F_T/\gamma_{k+3}(F_T),\quad K=\gamma_3(U_{T,k}).
\]

The ordered IP markings by nonidentity involutions with graph T and order \(2^{d+m+k}\) are exactly
the quotients \(U_{T,k}/N\), where N is normal, contained in K,
\([K:N]=2^k\), and the images
\((N(K\cap(U_{T,k})_J))/N\) have IP for every pair J,L.
Generator-set equivalence is exactly the action of Aut(T) on these kernels.

*Proof.* Killing any set of generators preserves every defining relator of
\(F_T\). Collection in its class-two quotient gives at most d+m binary
coordinates, and (F0) realizes all of them, so this quotient is precisely
the marked model \(Q_T\). Every target has that same quotient by UA.Forest.
These are exactly UA.Nilpotent's hypotheses. Its all-parabolic condition and UA.Ladder
give the stated bijection and exhaustive search. An ordered isomorphism must
commute with the generating map, hence has the same kernel. A permutation
of the generator set preserves the noncommutation graph and extends to an
automorphism of \(F_T\) and \(U_{T,k}\); this proves the last claim. ∎

The last corollary is a finite classification procedure, with the marking retained. First form the finite group \(U_{T,k}\); then iterate UA.Ladder inside \(\gamma_3(U_{T,k})\), test the explicit intersection condition of UA.Nilpotent, and take the declared graph-automorphism orbits. The theorem proves that this procedure terminates and misses no target marking. Any reported numbers of groups, named presentations or universal orders still require execution and an application-specific identification. No numerical census is assumed or reported in this paper.

## 7. Affine square groups

Let
\[
 a(x,y)=(-x,y),\quad b(x,y)=(y,x),\quad c(x,y)=(x,1-y).
\]
**Proposition UA.Affine (the complete square presentation).** These generate \(W=\mathbb Z^2\rtimes D_8\), of type \(\{4,4\}\), with
\(ac=ca\). Its presentation is
\[
 W=\langle a,b,c\mid a^2=b^2=c^2=(ab)^4=(bc)^4=(ac)^2=1\rangle. \tag{S1}
\]
*Proof.* Put \(d=bab\), \(y_0=cd\), and
\(x_0=by_0b\). The relations give \([a,d]=1\), \([a,y_0]=1\),
\(dy_0d=y_0^{-1}\), \(ax_0a=x_0^{-1}\), and conjugation by \(b\) swaps
\(x_0,y_0\). Substitution of \(bc=x_0ab\) makes \((bc)^4=1\) equivalent
to \([x_0,y_0]=1\). Thus every word collects to \(x_0^u y_0^v M\),
\(M\in\langle a,b\rangle=D_8\). In the affine model \(x_0,y_0\) are the
unit translations, so these forms are distinct. This proves (S1). \(\square\)

The normal form also fixes our affine multiplication convention: the rightmost transformation acts first. The symbol \(D_8\) denotes the group of signed permutation matrices in two coordinates, of order eight. In all dihedral notation below the subscript denotes the order of the group.

**Proposition UA.Square (finite square seeds).** For \(n\ge5\), define the invariant lattice
\[
 \Lambda_n=\begin{cases}
  2^e\mathbb Z^2,&n=2e+3,\\
  2^e\langle(1,1),(1,-1)\rangle,&n=2e+4,
 \end{cases}\qquad e\ge1,
\]
and put \(H_n=W/\Lambda_n\). Its order is \(2^n\); the full linear
\(D_8\) coordinate is retained, even if its action on the vertex quotient
is not faithful. Its pair orders are \(4,4,2\). It has all Boolean deletions and IP. Its third lower-central subgroup is \(2\mathbb Z^2/\Lambda_n\), of order \(2^{n-5}\).

*Proof.* The lattice indices are respectively \(2^{2e}\) and \(2^{2e+1}\), so the normal form gives the stated orders. The three linear pair products have orders four, four and two; hence the marked pair orders cannot fall in either lattice quotient.

Write \(T=abcb\), \(U=cbab\), translations by \((-1,0)\), \((0,1)\).
The extra relators for \(H_n\) can be taken as \(T^{2^e},U^{2^e}\) in
the square case, and \(T^{2^e}U^{2^e},T^{2^e}U^{-2^e}\) in the diagonal
case. After deleting any marker, both \(T,U\) have order at most two:
deleting \(a\) leaves conjugates of \(c\), deleting \(c\) leaves
conjugates of \(a\), and deleting \(b\) leaves \(ac,ca\). Therefore
every lattice relator survives every deletion. The even Coxeter relators
also survive. Thus \(H_n\) has Boolean deletions and IP by UA.D.

Moreover,
\[
 \gamma_3(W)=2\mathbb Z^2,
 \qquad \gamma_3(H_n)=2\mathbb Z^2/\Lambda_n,
 \qquad |\gamma_3(H_n)|=2^{n-5}.                         \tag{S2}
\]
Indeed reduction modulo \(2\mathbb Z^2\) makes the adjacent squares
central; conversely \([[a,b],c]\) and \([[b,c],a]\) give the two doubled
unit translations, up to signs. Thus \(H_5\) is class two of order 32.
The squared translations are not always independent cyclic subgroups:
in \(H_6\) they coincide and have order two. Formula (S2) uses a lattice
index and does not require independence of those cyclic subgroups. \(\square\)

**Proposition UA.SquareDecomposition (the two square decompositions).** For every integer \(q\ge1\), let \(M_2(q)\) and \(M_1(q)\) be the groups obtained by adjoining respectively \((bcba)^q=1\) and \((cba)^{2q}=1\) to the complete presentation (S1). They are the square and diagonal lattice quotients, and have the decompositions (S3) and (S4) below. Here \(D_{2q}\) is the dihedral group of order \(2q\), including \(D_2=C_2\) and \(D_4=C_2^2\). 

*Proof.* Here bcba is translation by \((1,0)\), and \((cba)^2\) is translation by \((1,1)\). Their signed-permutation orbits therefore generate exactly the square and diagonal lattices in (S3)–(S4). This identifies those complete presentations with the indicated lattice quotients.

In the square case, \(\langle a,c^b\rangle\) acts on the first coordinate and \(\langle a^b,c\rangle\) on the second. Their translations have order q; each is \(D_{2q}\), since its reflection has nonidentity linear part, also when q=1. They commute and intersect trivially. The reflection b interchanges the two factors and lies outside their product. They and b generate all three original generators. Hence
\[
 W/q\mathbb Z^2\cong(D_{2q}\times D_{2q})\rtimes C_2.
 \tag{S3}
\]

In the diagonal case, put \(A_0=\langle b^a,b^c\rangle\) and \(B_0=\langle b,b^{ac}\rangle\). Their reflecting axes are respectively \(x+y=0,1\) and \(x-y=0,-1\). The first factor has translations along \((1,1)\), the second along \((1,-1)\), of order q in each case. Each reflection retains its nonidentity linear part, so the two factors have order 2q also at q=1. All cross-generators commute. Their linear parts show that an element of their intersection must be a translation; the independence of the two diagonal translations modulo the defining lattice then makes that intersection trivial. Thus \(A_0B_0\cong D_{2q}\times D_{2q}\).

Both a and c exchange the two factors by conjugation, so normalize their product. The two individual reflections a,c do not belong to the product by their linear parts. The only remaining possible nontrivial intersection with \(\langle a,c\rangle\) is ac. Every translation part of an element of \(A_0B_0\) with linear part \(-1\) has coordinates of the same parity, whereas ac has translation part \((0,1)\). The diagonal lattice preserves this parity condition, so ac is also outside the product. Finally b already lies in \(B_0\), so the two factors and \(\langle a,c\rangle\) generate the full group. Consequently
\[
 W/q\langle(1,1),(1,-1)\rangle
 \cong(D_{2q}\times D_{2q})\rtimes(C_2\times C_2).
 \tag{S4}
\]
These arguments specify the actions by the original affine generators and prove the decompositions, rather than only equality of orders. \(\square\)

## 8. Changing the orders on selected pairs

**Theorem UA.Edge (dihedral attachments).** Let \(B=\langle b_i:i\in I\rangle\) be marked by nonidentity involutions and have every Boolean deletion. Select a family \(\mathcal E\) of distinct pairs \(S=\{i,j\}\) for which the product order \(q_S=|b_i b_j|\) is finite and even. For each selected pair choose a positive multiple \(m_S\) of \(q_S\), and attach the marked dihedral quotient
\[
D_{2m_S}\longrightarrow B_S=D_{2q_S}.
\]
The full compatible product of UA.SK is generated by its prescribed marker tuples. It has the intersection property and all Boolean deletions. Every selected pair has product order \(m_S\), and every unselected pair retains its original product order. If \(B\) is finite, then for every \(J\subseteq I\),
\[
|H_J|=|B_J|\prod_{\substack{S\in\mathcal E\\S\subseteq J}}\frac{m_S}{q_S}.
\tag{E1}
\]
If the base marking gives a regular hypertope, so does the constructed marking, and its full type-preserving automorphism group is \(H\).

*Proof.* A group generated by two nonidentity involutions whose product has finite order \(q_S\) is the dihedral group \(D_{2q_S}\); the normal form alternates between powers of the product and a single reflection. The indicated quotient exists because \(q_S\mid m_S\). It is injective on each proper local parabolic, which is either trivial or generated by one reflection. UA.D supplies IP in the base, and all input deletions are part of the hypothesis. Thus UA.SK applies. The pair supports form an antichain, so UA.SK.Local identifies every selected pair subgroup with its full local dihedral group. UA.SK.Delete gives the asserted deletions. The kernel on a selected pair has order \(m_S/q_S\), and (E1) is UA.SK's order formula.

For an unselected pair, each extra coordinate of its product is a reflection or the identity, because that coordinate's support cannot contain both markers. If the base product has finite order \(q\), its image under a deletion keeping just one of the two markers has order two, so \(q\) is even. The extra coordinates therefore do not enlarge its order. If the base product has infinite order, its lift has infinite order by projection. This proves the assertion for every unselected pair.

All local markers are dihedral reflections, hence involutions. Under the final hypothesis, UA.SK.Geometry gives the geometric conclusion and identifies the full type-preserving automorphism group. \(\square\)

## 9. Amalgams and primary torsion

Fix injective homomorphisms \(i_A:C\to A\) and \(i_B:C\to B\). Let \(A*B\) be the free product and let
\[
P=(A*B)/\langle\!\langle i_A(c)i_B(c)^{-1}:c\in C\rangle\!\rangle.
\tag{A1}
\]
We use the amalgam normal-form theorem in the following precise form: the canonical maps of \(A\) and \(B\) into \(P\) are injective; an alternating reduced word with factors outside the common copy of \(C\) has nonzero reduced length and is nonidentity; every element is conjugate either into a factor or to a cyclically reduced alternating word of length at least two, whose positive powers remain reduced with proportionally increasing length. This is a retained classical theorem.

**Theorem UA.Amalgam (universal property and torsion).** The group \(P\) in (A1), with its canonical maps \(j_A,j_B\), is a pushout of \(A\leftarrow C\to B\) in the category of groups: for every group \(D\) and homomorphisms \(f_A:A\to D\), \(f_B:B\to D\) satisfying \(f_Ai_A=f_Bi_B\), there is a unique homomorphism \(f:P\to D\) with \(fj_A=f_A\) and \(fj_B=f_B\).

Let \(p\) be a prime. Say that a group has \(p\)-primary finite torsion when every finite-order element has \(p\)-power order. This property passes to subgroups, to extensions with finite \(p\)-group quotient, and to amalgamated free products of groups having the property. If \(i_A(C)\ne A\) and \(i_B(C)\ne B\), then \(P\) is infinite. If \(A\) and \(B\) are finitely generated, then so is \(P\).

*Proof.* The free-product universal property gives a unique homomorphism \(A*B\to D\) extending \(f_A,f_B\). Agreement on \(C\) puts every defining relator in its kernel, so it factors through \(P\). The images of \(A\) and \(B\) generate \(P\); hence this factorization is unique. The equality \(j_Ai_A=j_Bi_B\) follows from the defining relations, completing the pushout property.

The torsion assertion for subgroups is immediate. For an extension \(1\to K\to E\to R\to1\), with \(R\) a finite \(p\)-group and \(K\) having \(p\)-primary finite torsion, take any finite cyclic subgroup \(\langle x\rangle\) of \(E\). Its intersection with \(K\) is a cyclic \(p\)-group, and its image in \(R\) is a \(p\)-group. The order formula for this restricted exact sequence makes \(|x|\) a power of \(p\).

For an amalgam, the normal-form theorem places a torsion element, up to conjugacy, in a factor: the alternative cyclically reduced word has powers of unbounded reduced length and cannot have finite order. The factor hypothesis therefore proves the torsion assertion. If the common subgroup is proper in both factors, choose \(a\in A\setminus i_A(C)\) and \(b\in B\setminus i_B(C)\). The words \((ab)^n\) are reduced of length \(2n\), so are all distinct; this proves infinitude. Finally, the images of finite generating sets of \(A\) and \(B\) generate their free product and its quotient \(P\), proving finite generation. \(\square\)

The simultaneous fiber product of UA.SK has its displayed compatibility equations and its proved generation theorem. It is not the pushout in UA.Amalgam. A graph of proof dependencies is neither of these group constructions.

## 10. Retained inputs, applications and attribution

The proofs use the elementary subgroup, quotient and isomorphism theorems. The further classical inputs are explicitly limited: Burnside's basis theorem for finite \(p\)-groups; their nilpotence; generation of the lower-central graded Lie ring by degree one and its multilinear commutator rules; and the precise amalgam normal-form theorem stated in Section 9. The Frattini, forest, finite-quotient and torsion deductions are proved in full above. No finite group classification, atlas census or numerical search result supplies a premise to these theorems.

For applications to abstract regular polytopes we retain the following standard correspondence [MS02, Section 2E]. A group generated by an ordered list of nonidentity involutions, with the string commutation rules and IP, is the full automorphism group of a regular abstract polytope with that distinguished marking. Its rank is the length of the list, its type consists of the adjacent product orders, and its number of flags is the group order. A generator-preserving epimorphism between such groups induces a rank- and adjacency-preserving covering of their associated polytopes. These are geometric input theorems, not new conclusions of the present paper. By contrast, the coset-geometric recognition and coordinate-kernel Helly statements needed for the hypertope branch are proved in Section 3 with their full hypotheses.

The supported-extension construction supplies a common proof interface for the two-power constructions studied in [HFL20, HFL19a, HFL19b, CFHS25]. Its use does not identify a compatible product with a universal presentation without a further order or normal-form argument. The forest quotient gives the canonical group underlying the main quotient and cover conclusions of [HS26], and the finite-kernel parameter space gives the exhaustive part of the order-1024 reconstruction associated with [GLP18]. Their exact source statements, literal corrections, numerical certificates and named-object identifications belong to the separate applications.

The elementary kernel, image and deletion proofs were already part of the reviewed proof library. The supported-kernel induction abstracts the recurring extension construction; the forest quotient and affine models were already proved in the reviewed two-group manuscript. We have retained those proofs and placed them with the shared theorems they support. The new work in this edition is the closed topic interface, explicit definitions, and the local clarifications recorded in the provenance ledger. Neither this reorganization nor the use of a language model establishes historical priority. Original source results and borrowed geometric theorems keep their attribution.

Existing Lean certificates cover declared components of the kernel, supported-extension and Helly arguments. They do not presently formalize this whole paper: in particular, the full forest quotient proof, the affine model and the geometric recognition/application interfaces are not all connected in Lean. Their written proofs above remain part of the mathematical obligation. The accompanying certificate scope distinguishes inherited checked declarations from any freshly checked application components.
