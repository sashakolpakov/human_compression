Chambers, universal constructions, and realization cones
Human Compression project
Abstract
We organize the mathematical content of Schulte and Ivi´ c Weiss’s 2006 surveyProblems
on Polytopes, Their Groups, and Realizations. Three common arguments treat distinguished
generators and closed flag walks, universal amalgamation with regular local types, and finite
equivariant realizations. Two oriented amalgamations are verified by their complete pre-
sentations. Previously established group results answer the sporadic existence question and
the regular alternating rank question within their precise scopes. The source’s remaining
classification inputs and questions are recorded separately. In particular, its two aggregate
counts for locally projective polytopes are reported claims, not reproduced censuses. All four
component manuscripts form part of the proof; this synthesis does not replace their proofs
by numerical summaries.
1 The source and the mathematical interfaces
The fixed source is arXiv:math/0608397v1, dated 15 August 2006, corresponding to the paper
inPeriodica Mathematica Hungarica53 (2006), 231–255. We use its twenty-five physical pages
and its complete TeX source. A later compilation date on the acquired PDF does not constitute
a later mathematical edition. This is a survey with thirty-four numbered problems, rather than
a paper with a single new classification theorem. Fourteen additional records separate embedded
conjectures, restrictions, and unnumbered research directions. They are not fourteen additional
independent conjectures.
An abstract polytope of ranknis a ranked poset with unique least and greatest faces,
the diamond condition, and strong flag connectedness. The diamond condition says that each
section of rank two has exactly two intermediate faces. Strong flag connectedness requires a
path between two flags that retains their common faces. These conditions concern actual flags
and sections; they cannot be replaced by connectedness of an unrelated incidence graph.
For a regular polytope, its distinguished involutionsρ 0, . . . , ρn−1 satisfy the string commu-
tations and
⟨ρi :i∈I⟩ ∩ ⟨ρj :j∈J⟩=⟨ρ k :k∈I∩J⟩.
For a chiral polytope, adjacent flags are in the two different automorphism orbits. Its distin-
guished rotationsσ 1, . . . , σn−1 satisfy the string rotation relations and their rotation intersection
condition. We retain the standard marked reconstruction theorems, attributed in the survey to
McMullen–Schulte and Schulte–Weiss. Relations without the intersection condition do not sup-
ply a polytope. The geometric reconstruction is an explicit antecedent of the arguments below.
The complete general proof for highly symmetric hypertopes supplies the corresponding
stabilizer argument. Paths within a residue identify its pointwise stabilizer with the subgroup
on its remaining types. In the chiral case even paths give the pair-difference subgroup. Polytope
flags are chambers of their face geometry, so the argument applies here. Conversely, a general
C-group or C +-group needs the geometric hypotheses stated in that proof. We use the marked
string reconstruction where appropriate, not an unrestricted converse for arbitrary diagrams.

Theorem 2.LetK, Lbe regular rank-npolytopes,n≥2, with compatible distinguished common
section. If any rank-(n+1)polytope has facetsKand vertex figuresL, there is a regular universal
polytope with these local types.
Proof.The full two-page componentUniversal regular amalgamation from a witnessgives the
argument, including the group-action justifications. Start with involutions on the rank-(n+ 1)
string, imposing remote commutation. Add the complete marked relations ofKon the first
ngenerators and ofLon the lastn. In a given witness the adjacency permutations satisfy
these relations at every flag: the local constituents are regular, so their marked relations hold
throughout each local flag set. The presented group therefore acts on all witness flags.
The two end subgroups identify with the exact local groups. Surjections in one direction
come from the local presentations; their actions on the corresponding local flags give the reverse
order-free identifications. An element in both end subgroups preserves the base facet and vertex.
Strong flag connectedness of their common section supplies a middle-subgroup element with the
same effect on the base flag. The free action of either end group makes these two elements equal.
Thus the end intersection is exactly the middle subgroup. The retained string end criterion and
reconstruction theorem give a regular polytope. Repeating the action on every other witness
supplies the covering map from this one, and proves universality.

in the source; a new proof of its common linear algebra is not a claim of priority for that theory.

Proposition 5.The full automorphism group of a finite neighborly regular or chiral polytope of
positive rank acts2-transitively on its vertices.
Proof.First suppose the rank is at least three. Given an ordered pair (v, w) of distinct vertices,
neighborliness supplies an edgeewith these endpoints. Extend the incident vertex-edge pair
(v, e) to a flag. In the regular case all flags lie in one orbit. In the chiral case, if the flag is in the
unwanted orbit, change only its type-2 face. This preservesvandeand moves the flag into the
chosen orbit. Do the same for any second ordered vertex pair (v ′, w′). An automorphism taking
the resulting first flag to the second takesvtov ′ andetoe ′. An edge has exactly two vertices
by the diamond condition, so it also takeswtow ′. Thus all ordered pairs of distinct vertices are
in one orbit. In rank one the two vertices are interchanged. In rank two a neighborly polygon
has two or three vertices, and its dihedral action is 2-transitive on them. Chiral polytopes have
rank at least three by definition.
This proves the necessary condition stated after Problem 33. It is not a classification or a
converse, and is not claimed as a new theorem in the subject.

7 Proof record and attribution
The three common theoretical components contain complete proofs: four pages for finite re-
alization cones, three for rotation conventions and flag walks, and two for conditional regular
amalgamation. The two finite examples add two pages with complete presentations, executable
GAP calculations, and independent literal permutation checks. All four have distinct reviews.
Their unabridged texts are appended to the reading volume. The earlier group and locally pro-
jective results are acquired with their full proof and certificate dependencies, not just their final
orders or page counts.
The new work here is the arrangement of these proofs, the explicit joins between their
hypotheses, and the short proof of Proposition 5. The source authors and their cited predecessors
retain attribution for established statements. The source register separately identifies definitions,
retained classifications, historical reports, acquired proofs, and questions. No new open-problem
solution is claimed. The computational checks are not Lean certificates, and no new compression
factor is inferred from this survey disposition. Its admission as a handled original requires the
separate review of this entire assembly.
