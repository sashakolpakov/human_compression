The remaining binary maximal omission
====================================

Working argument, not yet independently reviewed. This addresses fixed
L044 Proposition3.5. It does not claim a complete recovery.

Let H=G_i preserve two blocks of size m and be transitive. Suppose every
H_j=G_{i,j} is intransitive. Choose a distinguished involution l of H
interchanging the two blocks. Its orbits are m pairs, each meeting both
blocks once. Since l is even, m is even. Write C for the other marks
commuting with l, and R for the remaining marks. The string condition
gives |R|<=2. If R is empty, H preserves the l-pairs. Under a putative
r>=m, the following additional argument makes that pair system maximal
before the separately acquired pair-block branch is invoked. Choose
coordinates in the two binary blocks with l the row interchange. The
centralizer of l inside their setwise partition stabilizer is
C2 x S_m. The projection of <C> to S_m is faithful: its kernel lies in
<l>, while IP gives <C> intersect <l>=1. Its image is transitive on
the m pairs and has the inherited C-marking of rank r-2>=m-2. For
m>=10 the full acquired L047 transitive rank theorem forces that
image to be S_m or A_m. For m=8 the same conclusion follows from
the acquired complete boundary: all proper transitive exceptions have
rank at most five, whereas this rank is at least six. Thus the pair
image is primitive, and the l-pair system is maximal. The pair-block
branch applies. This missing maximality interface was supplied by
l044_resume; mere preservation alone does not justify that invocation.

The contracted object below is only a graph. An R mark need not act on
the l-pairs, so no induced permutation action of H on these vertices
is asserted.

For each mark j other than i,l, choose one actual j-edge whose ends lie
in distinct H_j-orbits. Such an edge exists because H is transitive.
Every H_j-orbit is a union of whole l-pairs. Contract those pairs to
vertices and retain the chosen edges, one per label. This graph F has
m vertices and r-2 edges. It is a forest: a cycle would give, for any
one of its uniquely occurring labels j, a path between the selected
j-edge's endpoints using all the other labels. Lifting the contracted
path by inserting l-edges gives a path in H_j, a contradiction. The
same argument excludes parallel chosen edges of different labels.

A stronger elementary consequence is useful. Any actual h-edge with
ends in two distinct l-pairs in the same F-component must join exactly
the two vertices of the selected h-edge. Otherwise the unique F-path
between its endpoints contains an edge of some label j different from
h. The new h-edge and the remainder of that path give a j-free route
between the selected j-edge's endpoints. Lifting again contradicts
the defining H_j-orbits. Thus any other unordered pair joined by an
h-edge must belong to different components of F.

For h in R, the actual h-edges join at least two distinct unordered
pairs of l-pairs. Here the two-block hypothesis and evenness matter.
If h preserves the two blocks, an h-transposition cannot lie within
one l-pair. If only one unordered pair of l-pairs were joined, there
would be one or two transpositions there; evenness forces two, which
interchange those pairs and commute with l. All other points would
be fixed, contrary to h in R. If h interchanges the two blocks, it
is fixed-point-free. If it joined only one unordered pair of l-pairs,
the matching would have to use both points in both pairs; elsewhere
it would equal l. It would again commute with l. This proves the claim.

If F were connected, the preceding two paragraphs would contradict
one another. Thus F has at least two components, giving

                         r-2 <= m-2, or r <= m.

Consequently any counterexample to the desired bound r<=m-1 has
r=m, exactly two F-components, and either |R|=1,|C|=m-3 or
|R|=2,|C|=m-4. This obtains the source's initial reduction without
its misleading assertion of an action of H on the contracted pairs.

Further local reduction at equality
----------------------------------
Assume now r=m and F has exactly two components. For any label h,
let its selected edge be ab inside one of those components. Distinct
additional unordered pairs joined by actual h-edges must cross the two
components, by the argument above. If there are two such pairs cd and
ef, join c to e in the first tree and d to f in the second tree.
These paths and the two new h-edges form a cycle. Every selected edge
on those paths must have label h, by the same omission-orbit argument.
The path in the component not containing ab must therefore have length
zero, and the other must consist of ab. Thus the two additional pairs
are ax and bx for a single vertex x in the other component. There
cannot be a third distinct additional pair. Consequently the simple
contracted graph of any h is either its selected edge alone, two edges
one of which crosses the component cut, or the triangle abx.

For h in R, only the last two possibilities occur. The actual two-block
action sharply restricts their multiplicities:

* If h preserves the two binary blocks, its transpositions lie within
  rows. The simple contracted graph is a union of two matchings, one
  per row, and so has no odd cycle. Thus it has just two edges. Each
  has multiplicity one or two. Evenness allows multiplicities (1,1)
  or (2,2), while (2,2) would interchange pairs and commute with l.
  It follows that h is exactly a double transposition, one actual
  transposition above each of the two distinct contracted edges.
  The transpositions may belong to different rows or the same row.

* If h interchanges the two binary blocks, its matching is perfect.
  At each l-pair either an internal h-edge uses both points, or two
  edges leave that pair. Thus its nonloop contracted multigraph is
  2-regular. If there were only one or two distinct edges, they would
  be doubled disjoint edges and h would commute with l. Hence its
  graph is exactly the triangle abx with multiplicity one on each
  edge, and h equals l on every other pair. On the three exceptional
  pairs, l*h acts by a three-cycle in one row and its inverse in
  the other. This description uses actual points and row coordinates.

These two normal forms replace an unrestricted pair-breaking mark by
either four moved points (row-preserving case) or a modification of l
on six points (row-interchanging case). They still require independent
review. For |R|=2, the two such involutions commute because their
labels are l-1 and l+1. Their possible overlap and the constraints
from neighboring C labels are the next finite structural problem.

Rank three for the two-neighbour graph
-------------------------------------
For |R|=2 let J contain all simple contracted edges of both R marks,
and retain all m vertices, including isolates. Let E_C be the m-4
selected C edges. Each edge e_j of E_C is a bridge in J union E_C.
Indeed any alternate path avoids label j altogether, since J uses only
R labels and E_C has only one edge of each C label. Inserting l-edges
lifts that path and contradicts the H_j-orbit condition. Thus adjoining
the edges E_C reduces the number of components of J by exactly m-4.
There must remain at least one component. In terms of the ordinary
graph rank m minus the number of components, rank(J)<=3.

In fact rank(J)=3. Each individual R graph is two distinct edges or a
triangle, hence has rank two. If their union still had rank two, its
nontrivial components would either be the same two disjoint edges, or
lie on the same three vertices. In the first case the unique edge
internal to an F-component would be the same selected edge for both
labels, impossible. On three vertices a two-colouring with a triangle
or two distinct two-edge paths has at most one internal unordered pair
unless all vertices have the same colour. The latter cannot supply an
extra edge across the F cut; in the former every R graph with one
internal edge must select that same pair. This is again impossible.
Hence rank(J)=3, and J union E_C is connected with each E_C edge a
bridge. This graph conclusion includes the row-interchanging forms.
