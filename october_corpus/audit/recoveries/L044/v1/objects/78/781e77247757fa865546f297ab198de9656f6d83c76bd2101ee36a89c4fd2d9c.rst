Two noncommuting neighbours: structural continuation
====================================================
Root working proof, not yet an accepted manuscript. The frozen contracted
forest component supplies the local forms and graph rank(J)=3. Assume
m even>=8, r=m, H=G_i transitive on two binary blocks, all H_j intransitive,
and R={r,s} flanking l in the actual string. Thus r and s commute.
The parent is a natural alternating string C-group with the previously
stated smaller-degree induction. The mixed/row-swapping four-pair cores
are assigned separately to l044_resume. No diagnostic is a proof premise.

The common graph and extra C edges
----------------------------------
Let Y be the union of nonisolated vertices of J. Every J component lifts
to one K=<l,r,s> orbit, of twice its size. The selected C edges are bridges
of J union E_C. After collapsing J components, they form a tree.
An additional projected C edge can use no other selected C label on its
path, by the usual lifted omission argument. Consequently it either lies
within one J component, or joins the same two J components as its own
selected edge. If either component is an isolated outside vertex, the
matching condition on the projected C involution rules out a distinct
additional edge there: that outside vertex is already used. Thus every
additional C edge has both endpoints in Y. Each C mark really permutes
l-pairs and has the form diag(pi)*l^chi, with one global row character.

Write c_minus,c_plus for possible C labels adjacent to r,s on their outer
sides. All other C marks commute with all K. Such a far mark preserves
the nontrivial J components individually: components of sizes3 and2 have
different K-orbit sizes; in the size2+2+2 case their marked actions differ
(r only, both, s only). It also preserves the union of outside isolated
vertices. Thus only c_minus,c_plus can have selected edges joining a
nontrivial J component to another component. A mark commuting with l,r
preserves the support of the contracted r graph, because that support
is the union of <l,r> orbits larger than two. The corresponding statement
holds for s. In particular an exit to an outside pair must start outside
the support of the R generator with which that C mark commutes.

Reduction to four pairs
-----------------------
Graph rank3 and the two local R forms imply |Y|=4,5,or6: the possibilities
for the nontrivial J component sizes are4,3+2,2+2+2. If |Y|=6, the two
R graphs are both pairs of disjoint edges, with one shared simple edge.
The three nontrivial K orbits are marked-inequivalent as above. After
collapsing all outside C trees as well, at least four vertices must be
joined by just the two possible core-joining labels. Impossible.

For |Y|=5, write P for the three-vertex component and Q for the two-vertex
component. Both R generators preserve the rows. Indeed, if one swaps
rows, its contracted triangle fills P and it equals l on Q. The other
row-preserving double transposition would have exactly one actual edge
above Q, which cannot commute with l there. Two row-swapping triangles
have union rank2 on the same support or at least4 when they meet in at
most one vertex; intersection in two vertices gives |Y|=4 instead.

The outside vertices form one connected selected C tree: otherwise the
collapsed graph has at least four vertices and only two available joining
edges. Its two joins are therefore c_minus,c_plus, connecting P,Q,O in
a tree, where O is the outside tree.
If the joins are P--O and Q--O, the outside path gives a noncommutation
path from c_minus to c_plus. Consecutive projected edges along that path
are distinct transpositions sharing one outside vertex; extra edges are
inside Y and cannot cancel their noncommutation. Together with
c_minus--r--l--s--c_plus this forms a cycle in the string, impossible.

Otherwise one join g connects P to Q. Suppose g commutes with r, so the
other join d commutes with s. The r graph cannot be entirely in P:
then r has a nontrivial <l,r> orbit over all P and only two-point orbits
over Q, prohibiting g. Thus r has one edge on Q and one edge on two
vertices of P. Commutation forces g to carry the entire four-point
<l,r> orbit over Q onto the four-point orbit over those two P vertices.
In projection it swaps the two vertices of Q with those two vertices
of P. The remaining P vertex v is fixed by g (the matching and the
absence of outside edges of g leave it no partner). Connectivity of
P in J forces v into the support of s.
If d joins Q to O, its Q endpoint is moved by g, so g and d do not
commute: g fixes the outside endpoint and d has no other outside edge.
If d joins P to O, it cannot leave at v, because d commutes with s.
Its P endpoint is again moved by g, with the same noncommutation.
But c_minus and c_plus are nonadjacent string labels and must commute.
This excludes |Y|=5. Hence J is connected on four pairs.

The case where both R generators preserve rows
----------------------------------------------
Two commuting, distinct double transpositions have either disjoint actual
supports, the same four-point support with different V4 matchings, or
one common actual transposition and two other mutually disjoint actual
transpositions. This follows by requiring one permutation to preserve
the two transposition orbits of the other (or exchange them).
If the two permutations have the same actual four-point support with
different matchings, all four points lie in one row: with two points in
each row there is only one row-preserving perfect matching, and odd row
sizes are impossible. Thus both use all four pairs, and neither c_minus
nor c_plus can exit to the outside. H is intransitive, a contradiction.

Suppose their actual supports are disjoint. If r uses four pairs and
both transpositions are in the same row, the four remaining points are
all in the other row; s also uses all four pairs, again prohibiting every
exit. If r uses four pairs with one transposition in each row, the only
row-preserving matching on the four remaining points is the row mirror
of r. The two simple contracted graphs coincide, giving rank(J)=2,
contrary to the acquired rank-three theorem. Finally, if r uses three
pairs, name it r=(a0 b0)(a1 c1). The available points are b1,c0,d0,d1,
forcing s=(b1 d1)(c0 d0). In this case
 (r*l)^3=diag(b c)*l=(s*l)^3,
a permutation outside <l>. This contradicts
 <r,l> intersect <l,s>=<l>.
These cases exclude disjoint actual supports without a census premise.

For the common-edge case, the three simple edges form a path: a star
would require three mutually disjoint actual edges incident to one
l-pair, which has only two points. A shared end edge and a shared middle
edge give, up to row interchange, reversal and renaming, respectively

 A: r=(a0 b0)(c0 d0), s=(b1 c1)(c0 d0);
 B: r=(a0 b0)(a1 c1), s=(a1 c1)(c0 d0).

In both cases the actual coloured K graph on the eight core points is
a path. In A its edge colours are l,r,l,s,l,{r,s},l; in B they are
l,r,l,{r,s},l,s,l. Neither path reversal preserves the colours. Thus the
centralizer of the K action on Y is trivial. Every far C mark fixes
all core points. Its global row character is zero, and its projected
permutation is precisely its single selected transposition outside Y.
The outside selected trees are paths, since three incident pure
transpositions would give a triangle in the noncommutation graph.

Exit possibilities in A
------------------------
Only c_minus can exit: r uses all four pairs, so c_plus, which commutes
with r, cannot exit. The exit is at a, the only pair outside s support.
The centralizer of the six-point alternating path for <l,s> gives two
possibilities, where x is the first outside pair:
 c=diag(a x), or c=diag((a x)(b d))*l.
There is a tail mark t=diag(x y), as the outside set has size m-4>=4.
In the second possibility e=(c*t)^3=diag(b d)*l=(s*l)^3.
Thus e belongs both to <l,r,s,c> and <r,s,c,t>. It does not belong to
Q=<r,s,c>: the set {a0,b0,x1,d1} is invariant under each of r,s,c, but
e sends a0 to a1 outside that set. This contradicts actual IP.
Only the plain exit and plain doubled tail remain.

Exit possibilities in B
------------------------
A left exit joins b to outside x. Its two possibilities are
 c=diag(b x), or c=diag((b x)(a d))*l.
The right exit is symmetric. The second possibility is already impossible
by IP: put u=c*l=diag((b x)(a d)). In A=<c,r,l>, the element
(r*r^u)^2 is a nontrivial pure row1 three-cycle on {a,c,d}.
The projection of <r,r^l,u> onto that row is S5 on {a,b,c,d,x}:
it contains (a c),(a b), and conjugating these by u gives (d c),(d x).
These transpositions form a connected tree. Conjugating the pure
three-cycle therefore supplies every three-cycle, hence pure A5 in
that row; conjugation by l supplies pure A5 in the other row.
Since s differs from r by the pure row0 double transposition
(a b)(c d), this puts s in <c,r,l>, contradicting independence.

Both plain exits cannot coexist. Write c=diag(b x), d'=diag(d y), with
x!=y; equality would make the two exits noncommuting. Each group
<c,r,l,s> and <r,l,s,d'> contains w=diag(a b). For the first group,
r*r^c is a pure row0 three-cycle on {a,b,x}. The row0 projection of
<r,s,r^l,s^l> is S4 on the four core indices. Its conjugates give all
three-cycles (u v x), hence pure A5; l gives the other row. Multiplying
r by a suitable pure row1 three-cycle gives w. For the second group,
use s*s^d' on {c,d,y} and the same argument.
But w is absent from K=<r,l,s>. On each core row let S4 act on its
three partitions into pairs, denoted ab|cd, ac|bd, ad|bc. In S3 write
p for the transposition induced by (a b), q for that induced by (a c),
and let phi be the automorphism swapping p,q. Both r and s induce
(p,q) on the two rows, whereas l swaps the rows. The graph of phi
is invariant under that swap, so every row-preserving element of K
has its pair of S3 images in that graph. The image of w is (p,p),
which is not there. This contradicts the required intersection of the
two end subgroups being K. Exactly one plain exit remains.

The omitted endpoint for the plain tails
-----------------------------------------
All remaining noncommutations are the full path of H, so the missing
parent label is an endpoint (otherwise the string would disconnect H).
In A, write O={a} union the outside indices. C generates diagonal S_O.
If the missing mark is next to s, it centralizes <l,r,C>. That group
has a big orbit of size2(m-2), on (O union {b}) in both rows, and a
four-point orbit over {c,d}. Conjugates of r under C, multiplied in
pairs, generate pure A_(m-2) on the first row of the big orbit. A
centralizing permutation fixes that support pointwise, since its
natural alternating centralizer is trivial. Transitivity forces it to
fix the whole big orbit. On the remaining four points <l,r> is natural
D8, whose centralizer is {1,diag(c d)}. Both preserve the binary blocks.
The missing generator would therefore preserve those blocks too,
contradicting the natural alternating parent.
If it is next to the far tail, it centralizes the subgroup with the
last tail mark removed. There is one big orbit of size2(m-1) and one
l-pair. Conjugates of r under the remaining C give pure A_(m-3) on
O' union {b} in row0, where O' omits the last tail index. Here m-3>=5.
The same centralizer argument fixes the whole big orbit. The only
nonidentity action on the last pair is odd, which is impossible.

In B orient the unique exit at b, and put O={b} union the outside
indices. If the missing mark is next to s, its commuting subgroup
<l,r,C> has a big orbit2(m-1) and the isolated d pair. Products of
C-conjugates of r give pure A_(m-2) on O union {a} in row0, so the
big orbit is fixed pointwise. The remaining pair flip is odd.
At the tail endpoint, use pure A_(m-3) on O' union {a}; again the big
orbit2(m-1) is fixed and the isolated last-pair flip is odd. Thus both
plain-tail configurations are excluded.
