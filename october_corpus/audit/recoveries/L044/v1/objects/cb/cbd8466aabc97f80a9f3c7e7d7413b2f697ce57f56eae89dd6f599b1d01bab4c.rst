Working review: common R2 reduction and row-preserving cases
===========================================================

Verdict and exact scope
-----------------------
I read the entire exact 781e7724 working note and independently checked its
actual orbit, graph and permutation-word arguments. The common reduction
to a connected four-pair core and the complete row-preserving continuation
are sound. No diagnostic was used. This is a working mathematical review,

Common graph and extra edges
-----------------------------
A J component gives exactly one orbit of K=<l,r,s>: contracted paths lift
using l, and none of those actual generators leaves the component. Every
selected C edge is a bridge of J plus selected C edges, as already proved.
The quotient graph obtained by collapsing J components is a tree. An extra
projected C edge cannot bypass any selected edge of another C label. Its
endpoints therefore lie in one J component or in the same two components
joined by its own selected edge. If one component is a single outside
vertex, that vertex is already matched by the selected C edge, precluding
a distinct extra edge. Thus every extra edge has both endpoints in Y.

The graph has rank three, so its nontrivial component sizes can only be
4, 3+2, or 2+2+2. A far C mark centralizes K, and preserves each nontrivial
component: their orbit sizes differ in the 3+2 case; in the 2+2+2 case the
restrictions have different distinguished kernels, being r-only, both,
and s-only. This last distinction is sufficient even if the common simple
edge corresponds to different actual row transpositions. It is a statement
about marked K orbits, not only abstract isomorphism types. Only the two
near labels can select edges connecting a nontrivial component elsewhere.

Six- and five-pair exclusions
------------------------------
For six pairs there are three nontrivial components and at least one
outside component (m>=8). Connecting these requires at least three selected
bridges, while only two labels are eligible. This is impossible.

For five pairs, a row-swapping triangle would fill the three-pair component
P and agree with l on the two-pair component Q. A row-preserving other mark
has just one actual transposition over Q, which fails to commute with l
there; hence it cannot commute with the first mark. Two triangles have
union rank two on identical support, rank three only on four support
vertices, and rank at least four otherwise. Thus both R marks preserve rows.

There is exactly one outside selected tree, since three or more components
P,Q,O1,O2 would again need three core-joining edges. If the two joins are
P--O and Q--O, the outside tree path induces a noncommutation path between
the two near labels: adjacent transpositions sharing an outside vertex
cannot have their noncommutation cancelled by extra edges confined to Y.
Combined with the actual string segment through r,l,s this gives a cycle,
which is impossible in a path graph.

Suppose instead g joins P to Q and commutes with r. If r's entire support
were in P, its six-point <l,r> orbit could not be mapped into a two-point
orbit over Q by g. Thus r has one edge on Q and one edge on two P vertices.
Since g centralizes both l and r and joins those four-point orbits, it must
interchange the whole two orbits. Its projected matching swaps both Q
vertices with those two P vertices. The remaining P vertex v is fixed in
projection: all other Y vertices are already matched, and g has no outside
edge. Connectivity of P forces v into s's support. The other join d commutes
with s, so if it leaves P its endpoint cannot be v. If it leaves Q the same
conclusion is automatic: its endpoint is moved by g. In either case g fixes
the outside endpoint in projection, and d has only one outside edge, giving
[g,d]!=1 by evaluation at that core endpoint. The two near labels must
commute by their original string positions. This proves the crucial Y5
exclusion without an induced action of either R mark on contracted pairs.

Classification of the two row-preserving marks
-----------------------------------------------
Commuting distinct double transpositions have exactly the three overlap
patterns stated. This follows directly because a commuting permutation
permutes the two nontrivial 2-orbits of the other, or fixes both setwise;
fixed points cannot be sent into a nontrivial orbit.

If the same four actual points are moved in different matchings, all are
in one binary row. Both marks then support all four pair indices, so every
near C mark, commuting with the other R mark, preserves the core support
and cannot exit. If the actual supports are disjoint and one mark uses four
indices, either both are single-row matchings and again no exit exists, or
the other is its row mirror and the contracted union has rank two. If one
uses three indices, r=(a0 b0)(a1 c1) forces
s=(b1 d1)(c0 d0). The cubes (rl)^3 and (sl)^3 both equal diag(b c)l globally,
including every outside pair, and differ from l. Actual IP then fails.

For a common actual transposition the three actual edges are mutually
disjoint. Their distinct simple contracted edges form a tree on four
vertices. A star would require three actual points in its central pair,
so the tree is a path. Shared end or shared middle gives exactly forms A
and B after renaming and row interchange. Their labelled eight-point paths
have no label-preserving reversal, so their marked centralizers on the core
are trivial. Far C marks consequently fix all core points and have row
character zero. They are exactly their one outside selected transposition.

Form A: twisted exit
---------------------
Only the near mark commuting with s can exit, at a. The six-point labelled
path of <l,s> forces its two possible forms diag(a x) and
diag((a x)(b d))l. All outside vertices attach through this unique exit,
and the outside path has a next edge t=diag(x y). In the twisted case,
(ct)^3=diag(b d)l=(sl)^3. Its global l factor, including outside points,
is retained. It lies in both <l,r,s,c> and <r,s,c,t>.
The four-point set {a0,b0,x1,d1} is preserved by r,s,c: r swaps a0,b0,
s fixes it pointwise, and c swaps a0,x1 and b0,d1. The candidate intersection
element sends a0 to a1, outside that set. It is therefore absent from the
required common subgroup <r,s,c>, proving the explicit IP contradiction.

Form B: twisted exit
---------------------
For a left twisted exit, u=cl=diag((b x)(a d)). With
r=(a0 b0)(a1 c1), r^u=(d0 x0)(d1 c1). Hence (r r^u)^2 is a nontrivial
pure row-one three-cycle on a,c,d: its row-zero part squares to the identity.
The row-one projection of <r,r^l,u> contains (a c),(a b),(d c),(d x), whose
edge graph is a connected tree on all five indices. It is S5. Conjugating
the pure three-cycle by these actual row-preserving elements gives pure A5
in row one; l gives pure A5 in row zero. The element r*s is the pure
row-zero double transposition (a b)(c d), so s belongs to <c,r,l>, contrary
to actual independence. The right-exit argument is its literal symmetry.

Form B: two plain exits and the S4 quotient invariant
----------------------------------------------------
If c=diag(b x) and d'=diag(d y), x=y would already contradict their required
commutation. For distinct x,y, r*r^c is a pure row-zero three-cycle on a,b,x.
The row-zero projection of <r,s,r^l,s^l> contains (a b),(c d),(a c), and is
S4 on the core. Its conjugates provide every cycle (u v x), hence pure A5
on the core plus x; l supplies its row-one copy. Multiplying r by the
appropriate pure row-one three-cycle changes its (a c) into (a b), giving
w=diag(a b). The other end subgroup obtains the same w from s*s^d' and y.

I independently checked the nonmembership invariant. In the S4 action on
its three partitions into two pairs, (a b) and (c d) have the same image p,
while (a c) has a distinct transposition image q. There is an INVOLUTIVE
automorphism phi of S3 exchanging p and q (conjugation by the third suitable
transposition). Its graph is a subgroup of S3 x S3 and is invariant under
coordinate swap because phi^2=1. Both r and s have image (p,q) in this graph;
l interchanges the coordinates. Thus the row-preserving part of K has its
image in that graph. The image (p,p) of w is outside because phi(p)=q!=p.
This is only a necessary membership condition in a quotient; no quotient
intersection property or faithful quotient action is assumed. Therefore the
intersection of the two actual end subgroups strictly exceeds K, as claimed.
For the manuscript, explicitly mention phi^2=1 rather than leave it implicit
in the words 'the automorphism swapping p,q'.

Plain-tail endpoint centralizers
--------------------------------
With a unique plain exit the noncommutation graph of H is a connected path,
forcing the omitted parent label to be an endpoint. In form A, O has m-3
indices. The near-s omission <l,r,C> has a big orbit of size 2(m-2) and a
four-point orbit over c,d. Products of C-conjugates of r generate pure
A_(m-2) on O0 plus b0. Its natural centralizer is trivial, and centralizing
a transitive group with a fixed point forces the whole big orbit to be
fixed. On the other four points the actual group is D8 with centralizer
{1,diag(c d)}; both options preserve rows, so the parent cannot be A_(2m).
At the tail endpoint the isolated last pair is the only possible support
of the missing generator; the other orbit has size 2(m-1), and the same
argument uses pure A_(m-3), with m-3>=5. Its remaining flip is odd.

In form B, the near-s omission has big orbit 2(m-1), isolated d-pair, and
pure A_(m-2) on O0 plus a0. The tail omission uses pure A_(m-3) on O'0 plus
a0 and has the same big-orbit/isolated-pair form. Both possible residual
flips are odd. The degrees quoted in the worknote agree with the actual
sizes of O and O'. These are pointwise centralizer arguments on actual
orbits, not estimates of abstract centralizer orders.
