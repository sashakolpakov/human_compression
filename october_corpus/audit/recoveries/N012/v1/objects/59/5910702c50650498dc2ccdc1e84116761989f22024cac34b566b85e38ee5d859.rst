Both centres and both quotients
-------------------------------
H contains each elementary transvection, so centralizing H or SL forces a
matrix to commute with every off-diagonal matrix unit. The simultaneous matrix
unit equations force a scalar, including in dimension two. The determinant
conditions give Z_1={lambda I:lambda^(2n)=1} and Z_2={lambda I:lambda^n=1}.
A determinant-minus-one scalar exists precisely when the congruence
n x=(q-1)/2 modulo q-1 is soluble. This is equivalent to d dividing (q-1)/2,
or e=(q-1)/d being even. In that case its image modulo Z_2 is central of order
two and has nontrivial determinant coset. It is disjoint from SL/Z_2 and
supplies all of H/SL, proving the direct product and the full-centre quotient.
The scalar itself need not have order two in H; only its image in Q does.

When e is odd there is no extra central scalar, and the projective map has
kernel exactly Z_2. The diagonal determinant-minus-one involution has image
outside PSL: a scalar moving it into SL would be exactly the forbidden scalar.
It therefore gives a genuine order-two complement to PSL in the image.
The proof covers all n>=2 and odd q; no simplicity assertion is needed for
this structural theorem. Odd n always has the determinant-minus-one scalar -I.
Even n alone does not decide which case holds.

Central extension of a rank-three marking
-----------------------------------------

trivial projection kernel would be the graph of a homomorphism G to C2.
Perfection makes that graph have trivial second coordinate, contradicting the
specified lifts. Thus the full direct product is generated.

two-involution presentation has the homomorphism sending both marks to z;

From generation to the four-dimensional quotient
------------------------------------------------
Since -I belongs to Z(SL4), each displayed image in Q is an involution and
the first two commute. They are nonidentity and distinct: A,B,AB are nonscalar,
while the determinant coset distinguishes G from A and B. The images generate Q.
For even e, the central-lift construction supplies the required marking;
the manuscript correctly refrains from asserting IP for this particular matrix
triple when a central cyclic normal subgroup is present.

For odd e, Q lies between PSL4 and PGL4. A projective centralizer of PSL4
conjugates each elementary transvection to a scalar multiple of itself. Its
sole eigenvalue is one, forcing the scalar to be one, and commuting with all
matrix units forces a scalar representative. Thus that projective centralizer
is trivial. A cyclic normal subgroup intersects the simple socle trivially;
their commutator lies in the intersection, so the cyclic subgroup centralizes
the socle and is trivial. All hypotheses of the retained rank-three criterion
are therefore checked.

Small determinant-sign groups
------------------------------
The common symmetric-square irreducibility proof uses all diagonal signs,
permutation matrices and elementary transvections, each of which belongs to H.
Consequently its actual proof applies to H, even though its previous theorem
was stated for GL. Averaging over the sign group is valid in odd characteristic;
its off-diagonal characters are distinct, and the transvection step creates
squares and mixed terms. Faithfulness is not needed for Scott's inequality.

For an admissible triple with ab=ba, the five factors a,b,ab,c,c have product
one and generate the same symmetric-square action. Scott gives lower bound
n(n+1). An involution of natural negative dimension r has symmetric-square
negative dimension r(n-r), also zero for identity. The resulting upper sums
are 5<6 in degree two and 10<12 in degree three. In degree four, equality
20 forces a,b,c each to have two negative eigenvalues, hence determinant one.
They cannot generate H's nontrivial determinant coset. This proves all three
uniform exclusions, including every odd extension field, with no finite search.
