Module B: Forest coordinates and primitive coset geometries
Abstract
We describe a common coordinate construction for several families of flag-transitive incidence geome-
tries. A positively weighted tree determines symmetric groups on its forest components and a laminar
family of set stabilizers. Unique paths compute all subgroup intersections, while a single atom-matching
argument completes every flag. Unit weights recover the inductively minimal geometries; contraction
of a distinguished edge gives the sign-kernel family of maximum-rank symmetric-group geometries;
and equal leaf weights on a star give the geometries defined by disjoint subsets and Kneser graphs. We
determine their primitive properties, establish the extremal marking correspondence, and compute the pa-
rameters and full graph automorphisms of the star family. The relative-generation and Frattini theorems
of Marked groups, kernels and intersections then give a second coordinate description: every firm resid-
ually weakly primitive geometry of a finite p-group is a direct sum of rank-one coordinate geometries.
The source statements are recovered with their retained classification inputs and with explicit corrections
to the unrestricted infinite Frattini assertion and to three boundary or parameter assertions in the Kneser
construction.
Introduction
The same incidence geometry can be described by subsets, by cosets of stabilizers, or by generators
whose omission subgroups are those stabilizers. Each description makes a different part of the structure
visible. Subsets make flags concrete; cosets connect the action to subgroup intervals; marked generators
reveal the intersection property. The purpose of this paper is to pass between these descriptions through
two coordinate systems, rather than to prove their common ingredients anew in every application.
The first coordinate system is a weighted tree. Its edges give cuts of a finite set, and deleting several
edges gives a partition. The symmetric groups preserving these partitions have exactly the intersections
dictated by the forest. The cuts are laminar, so pairwise compatible subset images have compatible atoms
and hence a common permutation representative. Theorem B.F supplies both the subgroup calculation
and the geometric flag argument. This distinction matters: a subgroup intersection property alone does
not imply that arbitrary pairwise incident cosets have a common point.
Three constructions enter this framework. The unit-weight case supplies the property tests for the induc-
tively minimal geometries studied by Buekenhout and Cara [BC98]. A sign kernel and the contraction of
one edge supply the second marking family in Cameron and Cara’s maximum-rank classification [CC02].
A weighted star gives, literally, the disjoint-subset geometries studied by Garcia-Colin and Leemans
[GL21]. The star specialization shares the entire existence and residue argument with the older forest
constructions. Its distance and automorphism assertions require further arguments, which are included
here.
The second coordinate system is a quotient vector space. Archer, Cara and Krempa [ACK05] showed that
the Frattini subgroup is invisible to firm residually weakly primitive geometries, and used this to classify
such geometries for finite p-groups. The general relative-generation, normal-subgroup ladder and coset-
transport arguments appear once in the companion paper [U]. Here we verify their geometric hypotheses
and obtain the actual coordinate form of every parabolic. The result is a complete classification, rather
than only an order or rank bound.
The symmetric-group converse retains two results of Whiston, including the separate omission-subgroup
consequence of his classification-dependent argument. The inductively minimal converse retains the ear-
lier Buekenhout–Cara–Dehon classification. These inputs are stated precisely below. The constructions,
flag completion, geometric recognition and parameter proofs do not presume the conclusions being re-
covered.

A typed incidence geometry has a reflexive symmetric incidence relation, with distinct elements of the
same type never incident. A flag is a pairwise incident set; a chamber has every type. Every flag of a
geometry is required to extend to a chamber. Firmness means that every nonchamber flag extends to at
least two chambers. A residue consists of the elements incident with a flag whose types do not occur
in that flag. Residual connectedness requires connected incidence graphs in residues of rank at least
two. We keep the specified group action as part of the pair. The [BC98]/[CC02] source coset convention
includes flag transitivity; for the firm-RWPRI assertions it follows from Lemma B.Flags below.

The diagram has one vertex for each type; two vertices are joined when their rank-two residue is not

Lemma B.Flags (residual weak primitivity and flags). A finite-rank geometry on which a group acts
residually weakly primitively is flag-transitive.
Proof. Fix a chamber. Starting with its empty flag, choose a remaining type on which that flag’s stabi-
lizer acts primitively, hence transitively. Given any second chamber containing the chosen flag, use that
stabilizer to match its element of the chosen type to the fixed chamber’s element. Repeat in the residue.
At most |𝐼| steps match any chamber to the fixed chamber. Thus the group is chamber-transitive. Every
flag extends to a chamber, so it is transitive on flags of each fixed type. The fixed chamber’s element stabi-
lizers then give the usual coset representation: the typewise coset bijections preserve incidence because
transitivity on incident pairs supplies a common representative. ■
Thus the firm-RWPRI results below need no extra assumption of flag transitivity or residual connected-
ness. For a flag-transitive coset geometry, firmness gives distinct 𝐻𝐽 , and RWPRI gives a maximal next
intersection at every proper 𝐽 . To see distinctness explicitly, firmness gives 𝐻𝐼∖{𝑖} > 𝐵 ; choose 𝑠𝑖 in
this difference. For 𝐽 ≠ 𝐾 , a type in one of their differences supplies an 𝑠𝑖 that lies in one intersection
and not the other.

3. Overgroups and the inductively minimal property tests
Lemma B.Overgroups (two-block overgroups). Let Ω = 𝐴 ⊔ 𝐶 , with 𝑎 = |𝐴| , 𝑐 = |𝐶| positive, and
put 𝐻 = 𝑆 𝐴 × 𝑆𝐶. If 𝑎 ≠ 𝑐 , or 𝑎 = 𝑐 = 1 , then 𝐻 is maximal in 𝑆Ω. If 𝑎 = 𝑐 ≥ 2 , its only proper
strict overgroup is the unordered two-block stabilizer
𝑊 = (𝑆 𝐴 × 𝑆𝐶) ⋊ 𝐶2,
which is maximal and satisfies [𝑊 ∶ 𝐻] = 2 . In all cases the subgroup interval [𝐻, 𝑆Ω] is a chain. A
positive weighted tree has at most one edge whose two sides have equal total weight.
Proof. The case 𝑎 = 𝑐 = 1 is immediate. The group 𝐻 contains the transpositions within each of
its two blocks. If an overgroup element does not preserve their unordered partition, its conjugates of
these transpositions include one crossing the original blocks. Together with the original within-block
transpositions the support graph is connected, so these transpositions generate 𝑆Ω. This remains valid
when one block is a singleton: a permutation moving that singleton produces a cross-block conjugate
from the other block. Thus a proper overgroup must preserve the unordered partition. That stabilizer
equals 𝐻 when the sizes differ and has index two over it when they agree. Its only further overgroup is
𝑆Ω, by the same argument.
If an edge bisects the total weight, the side of any other edge away from it is a proper positive-weight
subset of one of its halves. It has strictly less than half the total weight and cannot be a bisecting side. ■

Lemma B.Diagram (diagram recognition). Every connected graph satisfying the three diagram condi-
tions in B97 is the line graph of a tree, unique up to isomorphism. Hence B97 identifies every inductively
minimal pair with one of Proposition B.Minimal.
Proof. For a diagram with at least two vertices, make a vertex for each maximal clique. A diagram vertex
in two maximal cliques gives an edge between the corresponding clique vertices; a diagram vertex in one
maximal clique gives an edge from its clique vertex to a new leaf. Two distinct maximal cliques meet
in at most one vertex, since a shared edge would violate the unique-clique condition. Thus the resulting
graph has no loops or parallel edges. It is connected, and its edge labels are the original diagram vertices,
with exactly their original adjacencies.
A shortest cycle of length at least four in this graph would give an induced cycle of that length on its
edge labels in the diagram. A triangle would give three pairwise adjacent edge labels, which extend
to a maximal clique; its three pairwise edges would then violate uniqueness of the maximal cliques
already represented at the three cycle vertices. Both cases are impossible. The constructed graph is
therefore a tree. Conversely the maximal cliques in the line graph of a tree are its nontrivial edge stars,
so this construction recovers the tree. A one-vertex diagram corresponds separately to the one-edge tree.
Proposition B.Minimal constructs an inductively minimal pair for every such diagram; the uniqueness in
B97 identifies it with the given pair. ■

Lemma B.Johnson. If 𝑁 > 2𝑘 , every automorphism of the Johnson graph 𝐽 (𝑁 , 𝑘)is induced by a
unique permutation of [𝑁 ].
Proof. The vertices are the 𝑘-sets, with adjacency when their intersection has size 𝑘 − 1. For 𝑘 = 1 this
is the complete graph on [𝑁 ], so the assertion holds. Assume 𝑘 ≥ 2 . For each (𝑘 − 1)-set 𝐶, the family
12
of 𝑘-sets containing 𝐶 is a clique, called its star. For each (𝑘 + 1)-set 𝐷, the family of 𝑘-sets contained
in 𝐷 is a clique, called its top.
These are all maximal cliques. To see this, take two adjacent vertices 𝐴 = 𝐶 ∪ {𝑎} , 𝐵 = 𝐶 ∪ {𝑏} . Any
further vertex adjacent to both either contains 𝐶, or has the form (𝐶 ∖ {𝑥}) ∪ {𝑎, 𝑏} for some 𝑥 ∈ 𝐶 .
In the latter case it is contained in 𝐷 = 𝐶 ∪ {𝑎, 𝑏} . A vertex 𝐶 ∪ {𝑧} with 𝑧 ∉ 𝐷 meets such a latter
vertex in only 𝑘 − 2 points, so cannot occur in the same clique. Consequently a clique through 𝐴, 𝐵 lies
entirely in their star or their top, and maximality gives the whole star or top. Both displayed families are
maximal by the same observation.
Stars have size 𝑁 − 𝑘 + 1, larger than the top size 𝑘 + 1; hence every automorphism preserves stars. Two
distinct stars intersect if and only if their (𝑘 − 1)-sets differ in one point, so the induced action on stars is
an automorphism of 𝐽 (𝑁 , 𝑘 − 1). Induction identifies it with a permutation of [𝑁 ], since 𝑁 > 2(𝑘 − 1).
A 𝑘-set is determined by the stars containing it, namely its (𝑘 − 1)-subsets. The action on stars therefore
determines the original action, proving existence and uniqueness. ■
